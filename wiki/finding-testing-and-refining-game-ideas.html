Finding, Testing, and Refining Game Ideas

How game literacy, varied inputs, recombined influences, practical constraints, creative habits, and public prototypes help developers generate, select, and refine ideas.

Creativity as deliberate recombination

In a January 2014 talk, Raph Koster rejects creativity as ideas arriving fully formed through inspiration. He describes it as an intentional skill: existing patterns collide, move into unfamiliar contexts, and change in the process. He says research associates creativity with scenes, cities, and mixtures of cultures, subcultures, and disciplines, and that non-homogeneous teams tend to be more creative than homogeneous teams or teams that have worked together for a long time.

For Koster, personal expression remains valuable but is not necessarily innovative. Creativity depends on making unusual connections and can be practiced until it becomes habitual. His detailed methods for finding and transforming underlying rule structures appear in Game Mechanics, Variants, and Transformations; exercises for cultivating the habit appear in Learning Game Development Through Constraints and Experiments.

Develop judgment by studying many games

Payne Robinson attributes much of his judgment about game ideas to playing widely and repeatedly examining why games work. As a child, he read gaming magazines and reviews even for games he did not play. He compared different reviewers’ reactions, followed their references to earlier games, and investigated why one implementation was considered better than another.

He compares this process with a documentary account of Quentin Tarantino watching and studying the films available through the video store where he worked. In Robinson’s analogy, a large repertoire supplies techniques, characters, situations, and structures that can later be brought together. Some borrowing is conscious, while other influences become apparent only in retrospect.

Koster similarly treats disciplinary knowledge as the substrate for creativity. He recommends studying how games are assembled and looking past genre surfaces to their rules, behaviors, resources, mathematics, and component mechanics. Broad reading and varied experience add material from outside games, while explaining a system to someone else forces it to be abstracted and reduced to its operative relationships.

Robinson’s literature background similarly trained him to recognize relationships and recurring patterns across works. He describes taste as cumulative: judgments about whether a mechanic or aesthetic feels right reflect thousands of hours of play and the influence of many designers, even when the judgment cannot be reduced to a formula.

His earlier music-production experience supplied another form of repetition. After making thousands of songs, he became more comfortable distinguishing an idea worth finishing from one unlikely to become a satisfying song. He applies the same principle to games: unfinished experiments can develop the ability to discard weak ideas and identify stronger ones. This differs from advice that every game must be completed.

Recombine influences rather than copy one game

Robinson identifies Plague Inc., Destroy All Humans, Defend Your Castle, Stick RPG, and possibly Interactive Buddy among the influences visible in Execute. These games did not supply one template. He describes bringing together parts of several experiences into a new combination.

Defend Your Castle illustrates the distinction between copying a mechanic and extracting the experience beneath it. Its physics-based mouse interaction differs from Execute, but Robinson recognized that exerting visceral power over small figures was enjoyable. Because physics programming was outside his preferred skill set, he sought a technically simpler way to produce that feeling.

For Medium Rare, Robinson began with another experience he personally wanted: a first-person, turn-based dungeon-crawler and extraction game whose influences included Resident Evil, Witchfire, 3D rasterized graphics, and real-time graphics reduced to a stepped frame rate compared with animated Spider-Man films. He says he did not know of currently successful dungeon crawlers or turn-based games outside very large productions, naming the title transcribed as Clare Obscure as an “indie-ish” exception. He resisted turning Medium Rare into the auto-battling incremental or roguelike that he considered a safer market choice. He accepts that combining every favorite element can produce an overscoped mess; his qualification is that experience making and shipping several small games helped him constrain this combination, and that small scope was part of why he selected it.

His preferred approach is to understand multiple formulas, combine them, and add an idea of his own. Established formulas can produce a competent, familiar game; Robinson reserves his strongest praise for games that recombine influences into something players have not seen before.

Koster describes the same broad process as removing a pattern from its comfortable context. A tool, mechanic, metaphor, or relationship can be made unfamiliar and combined with something that initially appears unrelated. He presents collisions among people, subjects, places, and physical materials as ways to provoke those transfers rather than waiting for an idea to appear spontaneously.

Reuse conventions where novelty has little value

Broad game literacy also informs small interaction decisions. Both Robinson and Emerson argue that developers should reuse familiar solutions when a new convention would only create friction. Their examples include following a widely understood inventory pattern such as Minecraft’s, using Escape as the obvious PC route to pause a game, and keeping settings reachable during play rather than requiring several steps back to the main menu.

This borrowing is distinct from copying a game’s identity. A developer can preserve conventional controls and interface expectations while using novelty for the mechanic, fantasy, or structure that differentiates the project.

Koster separately treats deliberately changing an input as a possible source of meaningful variants when the control itself is the structure being explored. The sources leave his input experiments beside Robinson and Emerson’s preference for familiar conventions where novelty would only create friction.

Draw thematic ideas from specific experiences

Koster considers untold thematic premises comparatively easy to find once designers step away from familiar game subjects, and says designers can also look to stories in other media. He recommends looking at a vacation, childhood experience, or peak emotional moment. Audience suggestions included learning to ride a bicycle and adopting a child. While judging awards, he says the similarity became visible when he laid game covers together and repeatedly saw a stubbled, shaved-headed, bearded man carrying a large gun.

His criticism is not only visual. He argues that many games return to becoming more powerful rather than asking what an experience is actually about. His analysis of Die Hard treats reconnection with the wife transcribed as the protagonist’s “arranged wife,” rather than the terrorists and explosions, as its emotional core; in his reading, the protagonist is willing even to walk on broken glass for that objective. The corresponding task of finding rules that carry the intended meaning is covered in Focused Game Design, Progression, and Player Agency.

Match the idea to personal taste and available capability

Robinson says he must like a game himself, but he also checks whether other people respond to it. Selection therefore includes both personal interest and external testing. When developers treat players as an unknowable external audience, his proposed starting question is what they themselves would want to play.

He distinguishes that priority from following active genres, copying a working structure, and changing or reskinning it slightly. He names incrementals, “node busters,” and “friend slop” among forms developers may be encouraged to follow, and does not reject that approach when revenue is the primary objective. His own stated objective is work he enjoys playing, is proud of, and finds exciting. Execute was chosen not merely because incrementals were popular, but because he liked them and wanted to make a small project.

He deliberately filtered Execute through what he could implement well in his first commercial Godot game. Its initial boundaries were buttons, static sprite animations, and one currency, although the completed design grew to roughly ten currencies. Much of its apparent activity still came from buttons, static animation, and a ChatGPT-assisted shader that made purchased guillotines appear to multiply.

Robinson describes a recurring method of taking a game he likes but finds too complicated and condensing its satisfying loop. Factorio influenced the sequence in which a new machine creates a fuel or resource bottleneck, solving that bottleneck enables another capability, and the player repeatedly moves among connected production problems. Execute sought that factory-game rhythm without requiring players to construct complex automation or software-like architecture. Its detailed progression appears in Focused Game Design, Progression, and Player Agency.

Constraints are therefore treated as creative inputs. Rather than begin with everything a larger game could contain, Robinson asks what can be made fun within the techniques, time, and organization available to him.

An early Roblox project made with a friend provides a rough predecessor. In Space Scrappers, players flew spaceships around a track compared with Rainbow Road, collected scrap, and returned to a mothership after falling off or running out of fuel to purchase or upgrade ships and travel farther. Robinson says the team rendered everything at once, achieved only approximately 20 frames per second, and lacked established terminology, but approximately 10,000 people played it. Roblox tycoon games and their repeated upgrade structure helped lead him toward incremental games even though their embodied, world-based play looked different from older button-driven examples.

Broken, formulaic, and distinctive results

Robinson’s quality distinctions are personal opinions rather than general laws:

  • Inherently enjoyable: he thinks most incremental games begin with an engaging baseline because people enjoy purchasing improvements and watching numbers change. Text and rising numbers can be enough to establish that baseline.
  • Broken or badly received: an unfun game may fail to work or may make its core mechanic behave very differently from player expectations. He associates many examples with asset flips made by developers treating a market opening only as a gold rush.
  • Mediocre: a functional game can merely repeat what players have already seen. Copying Universal Paper Clips or making a generic platformer may work, but Robinson expects it to be unremarkable. In his estimate, making a terrible game is difficult while making a mediocre one is easy; he describes roughly 70–75% positive Steam reception as an accessible floor.
  • Formulaic: established skill counts, reward intervals, exponential curves, familiar structures, and a new visual treatment can target a dependable result. Robinson points to Fortnite and Call of Duty as games that systematize engagement. He recounts a video claiming that a Call of Duty map can present a target at a window every eight to ten seconds, while observing that annual releases are still regarded as approximately eight-out-of-ten games rather than perfect ones.
  • Distinctive: reaching what Robinson describes as the 85–90% range requires creativity. A game seeking still stronger reception, in his view, must offer something players have not previously seen.

Robinson does not reject formulas. Incremental development can be comparatively accessible because it can be built from buttons, and a developer who understands the genre can combine expected principles with an original twist. In that qualified sense, he agrees that it can resemble “easy mode,” while maintaining that the game must still be appealing and meet genre expectations.

He considers simple imitation less durable. Some unoriginal incrementals had recently sold well because demand for the genre was unusually active, but he would not stake his work on copying a successful asteroid-destruction premise. He cites diminishing response to later Assassin’s Creed installments as an example, in his opinion, of audiences tiring of the same underlying formula despite surrounding additions.

He provocatively says that studying enough games can make it difficult to create something bad. He also calls this confidence potentially privileged or hubristic, expects that a later project may fail, and accepts that most creators need to make poor games while learning. He reports that each of his earlier Roblox or itch releases attracted at least approximately 10,000 players, while Execute was his first commercial Steam game; those results provide the context for his confidence rather than proving a general success rate.

Make familiar forms surprising

In an August 2026 account, Robinson argues for games that are “weird and fresh,” but presents this as a personal theory rather than a demonstrated formula. He had produced one commercially successful game and explicitly says luck contributed. He repeatedly couples weirdness with freshness and entertainment, seeking an immediate reaction that makes someone who sees a thumbnail or trailer ask what the game is, investigate its Steam page, wishlist it, and ideally tell other people. His claim that a game without this “whoa” response is being made incorrectly is explicitly his opinion.

His examples retain recognizable game categories while changing how they look or behave. He presents Hylics as an older RPG whose clay-derived visual style made it stand out and reports that it had substantially more reviews than Execute. Cruelty Squad remains broadly a first-person shooter but uses an abrasive HUD and graphics, unusual play conventions, and a reload performed by holding the right mouse button while moving the mouse. Robinson attributes both games’ commercial response to their unfamiliar presentation.

The unreleased title transcribed as A Meaningless Random Numbers provides his contemporary example. Its underlying incremental structure still makes numbers rise and includes prestige, but its horror treatment includes shooting fish, anime characters functioning as perks, “sinning,” and apparently killing a character described as a mother of two. Robinson says its presentation compelled him to click and play, reports that it strongly outperformed other games in the most recent Steam Next Fest without supplying figures, and predicts that it will perform strongly after release. That forecast remained untested at the time of the source.

Robinson’s informal distinction between extending a genre and violating its expected presentation appears in Game Mechanics, Variants, and Transformations. The unconventional asset processes behind some of these visuals appear in Code Organization, Performance, and Technical Constraints, while the relationship between surprise and store-page traffic appears in Game Visibility, Steam Launches, and Creator Outreach.

Use itch as a low-friction proving ground

Robinson and Emerson describe itch as useful beyond adult games and game-jam entries. An unfinished or rough demo can test whether people will engage with an idea while also rehearsing the work of presenting it publicly.

They recommend treating a demo release as a small event without requiring final polish:

  • prepare and announce the page deliberately;
  • conduct limited creator outreach;
  • post to applicable communities such as Reddit;
  • use a revised or separate page to test another version when useful;
  • observe both the response to the game and the effectiveness of its presentation.

Robinson reports that one Reddit post brought approximately 40,000 players to the first rough Execute demo. Emerson says the itch version of A Game About Feeding a Black Hole continued receiving substantial play and that the team planned to update it to direct players toward the finished Steam game. The promotional role of these releases is covered further in Game Visibility, Steam Launches, and Creator Outreach.

Robinson’s strongest commercial test is that if players will not try a game when it is free and available with little friction, he doubts they will later pay for it. He abandoned Grid Tweaker because it did not attract enough players and because he did not believe he could execute it at the quality required.

He also reports, secondhand from a Jonas Tyroller podcast, that Bippinbits used game jams and itch performance thresholds to decide which prototypes should become Steam projects. Robinson recalls that the Dome Keeper prototype may have reached approximately 500,000 itch plays, but presents that figure as an uncertain recollection rather than verified performance.

Validation and finishing develop different skills

Emerson raises a counterpoint to repeated market tests: a developer can become trapped in “prototyping hell,” continually seeking an idea that meets a threshold without practicing completion. Finishing, packaging, and releasing a final product are separate capabilities.

The speakers do not resolve this into a rule that every prototype should be finished or abandoned. Low-friction testing can limit investment in an idea that attracts no response, while repeated completion develops skills that prototypes do not. The broader relationship among abandonment, project length, and release is treated in Project Scope, Risk, and Sustainable Development.
