Design Lenses, Coherence, and Creative Purpose

How design lenses, intended experience, mechanics, theme, form, space, and creative purpose can be aligned without prescribing a single design method.

Use lenses to change the design question

Jesse Schell’s The Art of Game Design, inspired by Christopher Alexander’s pattern-language work, presents more than one hundred lenses through which designers can examine their craft. The lenses supply a vocabulary for different approaches rather than requiring every game to begin from the same place. Shifting lenses can challenge assumptions and give a designer another way to examine a project that has gone astray.

Some lenses formalize discoveries that appeared through practice. The Game Overanalyser says Space Invaders introduced difficulty modulation accidentally and connects the effect with Lens 21 and flow theory. Pac-Man popularized power-ups and AI state machines; Lens 28 asks designers to consider the new mechanics and objects introduced into a game. Super Mario Bros. combined escalating difficulty, power-ups, and mechanics with Shigeru Miyamoto’s player-first approach. The source associates Miyamoto’s engineering of fun with Lens 5 while also emphasizing his insistence that games should be joyful to play. It names Steve Swink’s Game Feel and Raph Koster’s A Theory of Fun as later formalizations of ideas about feel and fun.

Begin from an intended experience

Lens 1 asks the designer to understand the emotional experience being built for the player and then construct the game around it. During the development of Subnautica, its designers explicitly used this lens to move the game toward horror because they identified terror as a core emotion of scavenging beneath the sea. The Game Overanalyser presents Jenova Chen as a prominent exponent of emotional design through games such as Flower and Journey.

This experience-first approach contrasts with a historical tendency to begin with mechanics and fun, but neither starting point determines the whole design. A project may begin from mechanics, theme, or emotion. The lens of unification instead asks whether its components become tightly aligned.

The Mechanics–Dynamics–Aesthetics framework is presented as another way to consider why people play and then construct an experience around those reasons. Schell’s Lens 30, the lens of emergence, asks designers to treat games as systems capable of generating strategic or narrative possibilities. Lenses 26 through 33 are said to examine relationships among mechanics, objects, systems, and emergence. The exact titles of the additional books named in this part of the transcript are not consistently intelligible.

Concentrate everything around the core

Payne Robinson argues that a game with a strong central idea should remove additions that do not serve it. In his terminology, a “perfect” game is one in which every part reinforces that core. This is an attributed design preference rather than a universal definition of quality.

Universal Paper Clips is his model. He describes it as resembling a spreadsheet, possibly written in JavaScript or Python, with Times New Roman text and generic buttons. Its subject is making paper clips, and every button helps the player make more paper clips. Despite consisting only of text and numbers, it held his attention for hours and became, in his opinion, one of the best games he had played. Its presentation demonstrated that a focused mechanical idea could remain engaging after nearly everything else had been stripped away.

Use small scope to focus the experience

After playing approximately 10 of UFO 50’s 50 games in September 2024, Robinson argued that limitations can emphasize the parts of a game the designer wants players to notice and keep attention on its fun. He did not claim that every entry was a “perfect gem,” and his assessment concerned experiential focus rather than measured reductions in budget or development time.

Paint Chase is his clearest example. He describes its premise as resembling Splatoon with a car and recalls older Namco compilations, though he considers it somewhat more intricate. The player drives to paint as much of the screen as possible. Rival cars do not damage the player and can be run through or over; collision chiefly matters for environmental obstacles, and the game includes only a small number of power-ups. Its stated extent is 25 courses followed by defeating the pink racer. For Robinson, excluding additional combat interactions and fixing the number of courses keeps the design centered on painting and its selected obstacles.

Although the collection deliberately presents many small games, Robinson says several feel capable of standing as full indie projects. He attributes that impression partly to their limited scope and partly to their sprite work, interface design, and seamless flow. During his brief time with the collection, he had already spent several hours with a couple of entries.

One unnamed entry supplies a second example. A town contains people trying to survive, and the player places bombs so that detonating one slowly triggers a chain reaction through the others. Robinson says he played for two hours and still wanted to continue, comparing its pull with 2048 or Tetris. The transcript does not make the game’s success and failure condition intelligible, so that condition cannot be reconstructed.

His resulting recommendation is to keep close control over what a game is intended to be, cut elements that do not work, and add something only when it serves the gameplay loop and overall design. His contrasting treatment of additive and transformative changes to Snake appears in Game Mechanics, Variants, and Transformations.

Move between mechanics and theme

Raph Koster considers both experience-first and mechanics-first design valid, but warns against stopping at either end. Starting with an intended experience can lead back to familiar mechanics whose existing associations undermine the intended point. Starting with a novel rule can lead to an arbitrary skin. He recommends moving between the two until the mathematical relationships and subject correspond.

From the mechanics side, the designer can ask whether a relationship implies teamwork, persuasion, radicalization, friendship, or something else. Koster’s example uses two units that can move successfully only when they remain near and linked to each other. He describes the mechanic as friendship, gives the game title transcribed as “eco” as an example, and then asks what larger system depends on friendship. It might become part of a story in which a parent teaches a child to ride a bicycle.

From the experience side, Koster describes a design exercise about romance. Drawing on his wife’s work toward becoming a romance novelist, he characterizes the form as two people with flaws who help one another overcome them—the “gift of completion.” The design moved between that theme and a gifting mechanic: characters must learn about one another and give resources without knowing how much the other already has, while confidence, independence, and commitment become the resources. Koster says the resulting unnamed game was fairly successful but provides no metric.

He associates AAA design with beginning from the thematic top and casual or puzzle design with beginning from the mechanical bottom and applying a skin. His position is that especially interesting innovation probably changes both levels together, not that either starting point is invalid.

Align fantasy, tone, and representation

Robinson draws on Jonas Tyroller’s advice that a good game sells a fantasy. He describes Execute as offering a disturbing but recognizable destruction fantasy: a child who builds a block tower or elaborate Lego set may also want to crush it. The game asks what it would be like to destroy the world without consequences.

The handling of that fantasy is deliberately simplified. The result is gory and “messed up,” in Robinson’s description, but remains cartoony enough for the player to suspend the atrocities being performed. He also regards nostalgia as part of the game’s appeal. The fantasy, cartoon treatment, art, and nostalgic quality operate together rather than as separable mechanical rules.

Abstracting the victims

Robinson deliberately avoided depicting the victims as one identifiable group. His Brazilian artist initially drew a blue victim and red executioner. Robinson rejected that red-versus-blue division while noting that he lived in the United States and expected it to be an important market. He suggested gray figures; the artist preferred blue visually, and the figures became one blue group.

Players asked for randomly generated names or identifiable people. Robinson rejected that specificity because the game concerns eliminating the population and he did not want to represent the victims as particular real people.

He implemented but withheld a feature that could place an uploaded face on a victim. He believed the resulting images could be highly shareable, but also expected users to target inappropriate real people. Emerson agreed that the feature had promotional upside but substantial misuse and reputational risk. Robinson had not released it at the time of the conversation.

Robinson believes the genocide framing discouraged some creators from covering the game, including creators who redeemed keys without publishing coverage. This is his interpretation rather than a confirmed explanation from those creators. The visibility consequences are covered in Game Visibility, Trailers, and Creator Outreach.

Adapt architectural patterns to game spaces

Some of Alexander’s architectural patterns suggest direct game-design questions. Connected play, identified as pattern 68 in the source, asks architects to create environments that encourage children to interact, which The Game Overanalyser applies to cooperative and multiplayer design. Paths and goals concerns guiding people through an environment by using conveniences and signifiers.

Clare Hosking’s discussion of architecture and level design supplies further examples. A chapel reference whose proper name is unclear in the transcript is said to have influenced Journey through monolithic structures, isolation, and a dark interior. Materials and decoration give BioShock and Mirror’s Edge contrasting atmospheres of oppression and emancipation. Architecture also gives way to city building and to the realized cities of the Assassin’s Creed series, where urban spaces are adapted for player interaction and engagement.

The objectives of architecture and game design overlap without being identical. The source therefore argues for adapting the spirit of Alexander’s framework to games rather than transferring its objectives unchanged.

Life and wholeness as design qualities

Alexander eventually used “life” to name an intangible objective underlying his patterns. He did not mean biological life, but an aesthetic quality that exceeds the sum of a work’s parts. He described it as making harmony from the interaction of humanity and nature, with the beauty of nature and the zest of life. The Game Overanalyser associates this property with truth, beauty, and wisdom and with works of science, art, and design. In this account, even nonliving objects can possess life, and creative work can bring more of it into the world.

For Alexander, a building’s beauty, life, and capacity to support life arise from its operation as a whole. Wholeness is a property of integration and unity of purpose. The temple transcribed as Tofukuji is presented as an example: it appears both cultivated and overgrown, reconciles humanity and nature, and integrates a designed path with its surroundings. The source describes insects, animals, natural rhythms, and the person walking through the space as forming a synchronized experience.

Fifteen overlapping properties

Alexander proposed fifteen properties that he regarded as inherent in nature and available to intuition and design. The source’s architectural examples include strong centers in a Cairo mosque and scale and local symmetry in a building whose name is transcribed unclearly. Schell translates the properties into game-design terms, although the source explains some more fully than others:

  • Scale reveals beauty or structure at multiple resolutions, illustrated with Pac-Man and Shadow of the Colossus; another title in the sequence is unintelligible.
  • Strong centers provide a central theme, idea, or sense of orientation, as in the opening of The Legend of Zelda: Breath of the Wild.
  • Boundaries establish where an experience begins and ends.
  • Alternating repetition appears in games through the modulation of tension.
  • Positive space articulates space clearly.
  • Good shape gives objects intentional form.
  • Local symmetries can operate within level design.
  • Deep interlock concerns integration among components and decisions.
  • Contrast operates spatially, temporally, tonally, and narratively.
  • Gradients are associated with flow, pacing, and movement across intensities or levels of resolution.
  • Roughness is named as an aesthetic lens relevant to art, design, and animation, but is not defined further.
  • Echoes occur when parts of a system, story, or theme reflect one another.
  • The void can provide reflection and recovery after failure.
  • Simplicity permits a small number of elements to remain intelligible.
  • Non-separateness concerns parts whose identities remain bound to a larger whole.

The Game Overanalyser initially groups simplicity, non-separateness, and boundaries with emergence; scale, alternating repetition, and gradients with flow and pacing; and strong centers, positive space, contrast, interlocking, and symmetry with spatial and temporal constraints. These divisions eventually break down because the properties can describe mechanics, story, space, pacing, or psychology at once.

A distant mountain in Journey draws the player toward the horizon. Negative space is proposed as part of the appeal of Facing Worlds. Contrast appears in Undertale through humor and tragedy, while Shadow of the Colossus uses intentional dissonance to subvert a traditional hero’s journey.

Echoes include matches between gameplay and story or the presence of a theme at several levels of a design. Schell uses Cuphead as an example: its frenetic act of jumping into danger parallels its story about two ambitious young characters entering a dangerous agreement. Schell also interprets this as echoing the game’s two developer brothers making a deal with Microsoft.

The void is compared with the “belly of the whale” stage of the hero’s journey, in which a hero reflects and recuperates after failure, but it also participates in alternating repetition and pacing. Go exhibits deep interlock because each decision depends on an opponent’s decisions. The title transcribed as eco uses its mechanics to reinforce a story about love and companionship. Shape may refer to spaces, characters, or the structure and conflict beats of a story.

Simple interactions can produce emergent wholes

The source uses Go to connect simplicity and emergence: a small ruleset produces an extremely large range of strategic decisions, making the game both simple and complex. Games more generally are presented as systemic constructions that emerge from a substrate of rules. The fuller mechanical treatment of emergence appears in Game Mechanics, Variants, and Transformations.

The Game Overanalyser compares rule-based games with accounts of biological life emerging from interacting chemical elements and feedback loops in Fritjof Capra’s The Web of Life and Stuart Kaufman’s At Home in the Universe. John Conway’s Game of Life demonstrates how simple rules can produce self-replicating patterns. Games can make large systems available for examination through history in Civilization, urban decay in SimCity 4, and biological evolution in Spore. The comparison is conceptual; the source does not claim that games are literally biological life or evaluate the accuracy of the named simulations.

Creativity as another emergent process

Douglas Hofstadter is cited for the proposition that creative thought originates in the recombination of existing patterns, analogies, and metaphors. Daniel Dennett is cited for an account of consciousness arising from recombined symbolic patterns until an agent can perform metacognition. These positions support the source’s comparison among emergence in life, games, and creative thought; they are not demonstrated within the transcript.

Artificial-intelligence research is said to have produced systems capable of musical creativity through recombination. ANGELINA is presented as an attempt to create games, although The Game Overanalyser finds its results less convincing. Schell speculates that Alexander’s heuristics for life might help give artificial intelligence a creative spark. The source presents this as speculation rather than an achieved design method.

Objective beauty remains disputed

Alexander’s proposal of objective standards for beauty has critics. Some argue that such standards stifle creativity. Others reject the premise that every work must pursue life explicitly. Art may be intentionally disjointed, dissonant, or profoundly unpleasant, and cultures may differ in what they consider beautiful. The Game Overanalyser does not resolve whether Alexander or his detractors are correct.

Alexander’s position is presented as something other than a top-down imposition of beauty standards. The Game Overanalyser interprets him as arguing that work arising from an authentic creative place will naturally converge on something more objective. Alexander connected the loss of that process to a reductionist view of the world and the commodification of artistic products, which he believed had damaged the perception of beauty and produced artifacts without higher purpose. This remains the source’s interpretation of Alexander rather than an established conclusion.

Form and function can lead in different directions

Alexander regarded the division between form and function as illusory. In nature, ornament has a function and functional systems can also possess beauty: flowers attract insects, peacock plumage impresses mates, petals occur in mathematical ratios, and symmetry appears throughout living forms. The source invokes Bertrand Russell’s description of mathematics as possessing truth and a cold, austere beauty like great sculpture.

Alexander argued that when a thing receives its nature from centers that support one another, form is function. Authentic creation would then produce forms with intrinsic beauty rather than attaching superficial ornament to a mechanistic function. He treated architecture’s function as producing and cultivating life, enlivening communities, and bringing out better qualities in their members. The Game Overanalyser suggests that other art forms may have more room to be subversive because they do not necessarily perform architecture’s social or practical function.

Miyamoto is presented as arguing that form should follow function and that a game’s function is to be engaging and fun. An avatar should feel good to control independently of the surrounding world. Levels can introduce abilities and then consolidate them incrementally, while signifiers and conveniences allow players to infer how to act. The first level of Super Mario Bros. exemplifies this approach. The source connects these principles with Don Norman’s work on affordances and human-centered design.

A contrasting philosophy, called design by subtraction, removes anything that does not reinforce the intended theme, tone, or story. Rather than beginning with fun, engagement, or game feel, the designer begins with an emotion or idea and shapes each facet around it. In the title transcribed as eco, the player controls a disempowered and persecuted child who stumbles and is difficult to control. Protecting a companion is used to produce love, intimacy, and selflessness. The designer’s and companion’s names are not intelligible enough in the transcript to restore without outside information.

The contrast extends to space. Miyamoto has emphasized building levels that accommodate the avatar so that the player-facing function dictates the world. The design-by-subtraction creator instead describes architecture as a world that appears to exist independently of the player, reinforcing isolation and disempowerment while supporting immersion.

Both approaches are presented as capable of producing notable games despite their seemingly opposite priorities. The Game Overanalyser concludes that a game can begin with either a functional mechanic or an aesthetic, emotion, or theme. Form and function can exchange priority depending on the intended design rather than supplying one mandatory sequence.

Play can serve plural purposes

Brian Sutton-Smith’s The Ambiguity of Play is used to argue that play has always been plural. Games engage different aesthetics, players, competencies, preferences, and predispositions, while culture frames what play is for. A single general theory of play’s utility therefore risks excluding some of its functions.

The transcript says Sutton-Smith identifies seven rhetorics through which cultures explain and privilege play. Its spoken list includes progress, fate, power, the terms “community” and “identity” in an unclear grouping, the imaginary, the self, and frivolity. Because the transcription does not make the seven-part grouping reliable, it cannot be reconstructed precisely from this source alone.

This plurality suggests that separate design lenses may lead toward different destinations rather than Alexander’s singular quality of life. Thomas Henricks is cited for a broader possibility that play perpetuates humanity through self-realization, participation in a social context, and becoming an integrated being. The source considers whether many functions might share such a higher purpose but does not resolve the question.

External structures can redirect creative form

Jonathan Blow’s talk The Medium Is the Message examines how commercial and cultural structures affect creative work. As summarized by The Game Overanalyser, early television sitcoms had to fit advertisers’ needs, while long-form content could not be explored as freely. Those conditions restricted pacing and storytelling. New platforms and serialized formats later enabled season-long stories and contributed to what Blow describes as a stronger period of television.

Applied to games, Blow describes free-to-play as fundamentally broken because its commercial constraints lead designers toward practices he regards as predatory. He also argues that large corporations control much of game production and that pressure to meet a corporate bottom line suppresses innovation. The indie space provides a partial contrast: budgets may be smaller, but creators may have more room to innovate when they are less constrained by outside demands.

These are Blow’s attributed evaluations as conveyed through the source, not findings about every free-to-play game, corporation, or independent developer. The common concern is that outside incentives may replace the intended experience as a project’s governing purpose. The production and business implications are treated further in Project Scope, Risk, and Sustainable Development.

Treat design principles as tools for a purpose

The Game Overanalyser’s proposed adaptation of Alexander is purposeful design: understand the kind of experience being attempted, identify its purpose, and use mechanics, aesthetics, spatial structures, narrative devices, and other tools to realize it. Design principles are instrumental toward that purpose rather than valuable merely because a framework names them.

This formulation accommodates engagement-first and emotion-first design, player-serving and player-indifferent worlds, and the different cultural functions of play. It does not require all games to pursue one emotion, beauty standard, structure, or form of play. The Game Overanalyser explicitly presents this as a personal estimate or guess rather than a demonstrated universal theory.

The source also speculates that humanity might be understood through Johan Huizinga’s Homo Ludens as a playful species existing in a universe capable of play-like emergence. This remains philosophical speculation rather than a practical design finding.

Creative purpose can reside in the process

Alexander wrote that living structure provides a substrate for freedom and for being free within oneself. The Game Overanalyser consequently suggests looking for life not only in a finished product but also in the design process. In this interpretation, life in design is the capacity to express a creative voice rather than a requirement that every product exhibit one prescribed quality.

The source compares this shift with expressionism’s movement of attention from the painted subject toward the painter and the process of creation. Blow’s concern about extrinsic forces is interpreted as a fear that artists will design around something other than what they want to make.

On this account, the lenses offered by Schell and Alexander can help designers recover an inner creative direction when commercial, cultural, or habitual assumptions move to the foreground. The Game Overanalyser explicitly presents this as his interpretation of the art of game design: creating a medium and process that allow designers to make what they intend and, in Alexander’s terms, to be free in themselves.
