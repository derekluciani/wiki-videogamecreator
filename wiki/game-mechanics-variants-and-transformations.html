Game Mechanics, Variants, and Transformations

How atomic analysis, mechanics libraries, recontextualization, and changes to rules, dimensions, controls, topologies, simulations, and metaphors generate game variants and new rule structures.

See games as rule constructs

In a January 2014 talk, Raph Koster describes a game as a construct of interacting rules, behaviors, and resources beneath its visual and narrative presentation. A new skin, such as a Lord of the Rings chess set, remains chess. Changing a rule creates a variant; accumulated variants form a family and eventually what is commonly called a genre.

His poker example treats an early game with fewer cards and no multiple rounds, later additions such as wild cards, and Texas Hold’em as members of one recognizable family. In a workshop example, an audience member proposed 2D platformers. Koster traced a family from Donkey Kong, through the reskin or clone Donkey Kong Jr., to Kangaroo adding punches and obstacles from the sides, and then to the side-scrolling of Super Mario. He proposed jumping to reach the other side as their shared core.

Koster claims that almost every video game is a variant and that wholly new video-game rule constructs are rare. He contrasts this with tabletop games, particularly what he calls German board games, which he says more often introduce substantially different rule constructs. His practical tools do not require invention from nothing: they can create a variant, extend a family or genre, or contribute to a new game.

Analyze games atomically

Koster argues that designers accustomed to large narrative productions may attend to a game’s surface rather than its underlying mathematics. He recommends revisiting simple games from the 1970s and 1980s and studying the casual end of the medium even when designing at a larger scale. He treats mechanics as games in themselves and larger games as arrangements of smaller games; a complete casual or mobile game can become a feature or subgame within a larger production.

To demonstrate the distinction, he abstracts Gone Home into four card piles, each containing an ace: cards are revealed until an ace unlocks the next pile. He calls the underlying mechanic environmental storytelling—finding hotspots until one unlocks another group of hotspots—while explicitly saying that this abstraction does not diminish the story’s value.

Mechanics may also be isomorphic: different mathematical descriptions can identify structures that operate alike. Koster says Sudoku can be viewed either as filling a map or as set packing. Item association and collection may likewise be forms of set packing, although he allows that they need not be.

Build a mechanics library

Koster compares disciplinary knowledge with the substrate that supports an aquarium. For game design, the substrate consists of studying games and learning their rules, resources, behaviors, component structures, mechanics, and mathematics. His strongest recommendations are to play many board games and mobile games and to play arcade games chronologically on the cabinet transcribed as a “mim cabinet.” He also names Game Design Patterns and Advanced Game Mechanics as useful collections of mechanical patterns, without identifying authors or editions.

This complements Payne Robinson’s account of developing judgment by studying many games, reviews, and recurring structures. Robinson describes taste as cumulative and recommends combining multiple influences rather than copying one game. Koster’s atomic method supplies a way to identify what, specifically, can be extracted and recombined. The broader process of generating and selecting candidate ideas appears in Finding, Testing, and Refining Game Ideas.

Move a component outside its usual context

Koster rejects the idea that a design appears wholly “out of the blue.” He describes creativity as moving a pattern from a familiar context into another one and changing it in the process. His non-game illustration moves clear glass through lenses and spectacles to microscopes, telescopes, and fiber optics; he explicitly says he does not understand why the imported plant ash in the first step reportedly made the glass clear.

Removing a die’s assumed role as a random-number generator exposes other uses:

  • a hit-point counter or timer;
  • an indicator of state or of a maximum value;
  • a physical building block, as in the tabletop game Blueprints.

A conventional card deck and an Uno deck can similarly prompt different directions because their suits, colors, and physical appearance differ even when Koster considers them mathematically similar. Cards can become a library of actions, a house, a fortune-telling tool, a convenient cover, or individual playfields. In an improvised workshop design, a face-up card became a dungeon room whose pips represented monsters and their initial locations; play would involve covering those pips.

Change statistics and dimensions

Add a rule, not only a larger number

Koster distinguishes adding “Fireball 6” from adding a genuinely new statistic. A proportionally larger attack against a proportionally larger enemy preserves the same relationship and offers the player nothing new to learn. A new metric can instead create a rule, goal, or variant. Tracking time can formalize time attack or speedrunning; comparing shots fired with shots hit creates accuracy. He says he had not seen an RPG efficiency meter and speculates that some players might enjoy one.

More statistics are not automatically better. When exploring a new one, Koster recommends removing a different statistic and examining the resulting permutations, while acknowledging that many will not work.

Extend the dimensions of play

Koster provocatively says there have been only five fighting games, although the transcript does not contain the complete classification. He begins with rock-paper-scissors as three attacks without defense or movement. In his classification, adding attacks only extends an existing statistic; Karate Champ adds defense as a new move class and rule, while distance becomes a new statistic. He characterizes many later fighting games as the same attack structure moving on one aligned axis even when rendered in 3D, calling the apparent third dimension a narrative or optical layer rather than a mechanical dimension.

The game transcribed as “Karatica” makes that axis infinite and produces a side-scrolling beat-’em-up family. “Battle arena to shindan” adds sidestepping, although Koster considers its control unintuitive. The titles inconsistently transcribed as “Bashidto” or “Bushidto Blade” extend movement across a full plane; he calls the result a bridge too far and says, to his knowledge, the approach ended with the sequel.

His platformer sequence similarly moves from a screen-bound space to infinite horizontal scrolling and then to a third dimension in Mario 64. These examples are Koster’s mechanical classifications rather than a comprehensive history.

Use analog materials to expose structure

Koster recommends analog thinking to make the rules beneath familiar digital presentation easier to manipulate. The full treatment of his physical kit, the turn-based abstraction of digital action, deliberate constraints, and repeated exercises appears in Learning Game Development Through Constraints and Experiments.

Add verbs and goals

Koster includes a new verb or goal among the elements that can create a variant. Goals can compete, overload the player, produce sandbox structure, or distinguish multiplayer roles. The full treatment and examples appear in Focused Game Design, Progression, and Player Agency.

Replace the input subgame

Koster does not accept “the interface is the game” as literally accurate, but considers it a productive design lens. Soccer Physics reduces a soccer game to one button that can be tapped or held. Other transformations can change movement axes or duration, divide one simple control into several, combine complex controls into one, or replace buttons, haptics, a joystick, or twin joysticks.

Controls can be mastered as a separate game. Koster describes the arcade controls of the title transcribed as “Robbitron” and a joystick-and-button version as distinct variants, and similarly distinguishes Defender with a reverse button from a four-way-joystick version. Proficiency with one port therefore need not transfer to another. Input is one of the easiest subgames to replace inside a larger game; his concluding example places Defender on the device transcribed as a “Wii mode.”

Robinson and Emerson separately recommend reusing familiar controls and interface conventions when novelty would only create friction. Koster’s position here is that deliberately changing input can itself be a source of variants.

Change the graph or topology

Koster recommends treating play spaces as graphs. In his examples, Asteroids occurs on a donut; Pac-Man uses a donut or Möbius-strip-like space depending on the level; and Snakes and Ladders uses non-Euclidean geometry with wormholes. Changing enemy movement in Space Invaders produces Galaxian, while joining the ends of a line into a circle produces forms such as Tempest or the title transcribed as “Gyrus.” Tetris variants can alter the pieces, playfield topology, or topology of the pieces themselves.

Workshop applications included putting FarmVille into a full 3D space, with hanging plants, multidirectional growth, or spreading plants. Another exchange treated MUD rooms as hypertext spaces with arbitrary nodes and links. Seeing tango steps embedded in an Argentine sidewalk then suggested a dance-teaching game whose steps form a graph. Parts of this exchange depend on unseen slides and a fragmented transcript, so the exact diagram cannot be reconstructed.

Mash up structures and simulation methods

Atomic analysis can reveal shared structures. One unseen diagram could be read as either Breakout or Space Invaders, suggesting a combination in which Breakout supplies the attack while invaders fire back. Koster characterizes Dance Dance Revolution as Simon Says with feet.

He also recommends trying combinations without an obvious shared structure, such as blackjack with “Robbitron,” or shooting robots with push-your-luck play. He does not claim that these particular combinations will work. The exercise is intended to expose a possible relationship.

A deliberately ill-suited simulation method can serve the same purpose. Rather than simulating an FPS with the most direct digital tools, Koster asks what a dice-only FPS might reveal: a roll might represent field of view, or dice might be rolled physically toward targets. The goal is not to reproduce the original simulation exactly but to discover a different way to enact its relationships.

Change the metaphor rather than the skin

A metaphor can add mechanically relevant relationships rather than merely replacing artwork. Reframing an FPS around cameras suggests depth of field, rule-of-thirds, and composition mechanics. In another partly slide-dependent sequence, Koster moves a visit-every-node structure among eating, painting girders, painting a house, and the title transcribed as “Cubert.” Painting introduces possible rules for jumping, repainting, and competition.

Changing the square graph to triangular or hexagonal relationships changes the play. Restricting visits to selected nodes turns the structure into object collection; hiding those nodes turns it into a secrets system. Koster claims that Pac-Man’s abstract visit-specific-locations mechanic was combined with Super Mario. These are attributed abstractions rather than independently verified development histories.

Mechanical transformation is only one side of design. Koster’s method for moving between an intended experience and rules that carry the same meaning appears in Focused Game Design, Progression, and Player Agency.
