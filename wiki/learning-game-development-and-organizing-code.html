Learning Game Development and Organizing Code

Using constrained tools and small experiments to learn development, organize interacting and reusable systems, and prepare a project to grow.

Basic programming versus code organization

Payne Robinson describes programming as a broad label for what often becomes a code-organization problem. A developer can learn to write a conditional statement or function, understand the basics of an engine, produce “Hello, world,” or move a character on screen by reading documentation and watching a few tutorials. The greater difficulty begins when a working project contains multiple things that must interact and continue working as more content is added.

Interacting and reusable systems

Robinson illustrates the progression of organizational decisions with enemies and save data:

  • Should every enemy receive a new script, or should one script hold the different parts of an enemy?
  • If those parts are reused across other enemies, where should the reusable parts be stored?
  • When those parts need to be saved, should their state go into an enemy-specific save script or a global save script?

The question is not only how to make two things interact once, but how to make that interaction work for all similar things added later. Robinson characterizes this as an unusual, abstract way of thinking. His recurring concern across projects is finding an organization that remains modular.

What tutorials can and cannot supply

No one has made the exact game a developer is making, so no one has already determined the organizational solution that best fits it. Robinson says useful solutions can be found for platformers, roguelike deck builders, and other genres whose common structures have been worked out. Those solutions can be used and pieced together.

He also cautions that following another game’s solution can make the resulting game feel like that game. Adding an original mechanic or twist can create organizational requirements that the tutorial’s project did not have. In his view, doing something mechanically different may therefore require an original way to organize the new behavior, even while established solutions remain useful for parts of the project.

When an experiment becomes a commercial project

Execute was not initially expected to become commercially viable, and Robinson says its code was not organized very well. After release, players requested updates, but adding them was much harder than they assumed because the existing organization had painted him into a corner.

His exact example is the global script: for the next project, he wants to ensure that it is not “a string-based dictionary,” because Execute works that way and, in his words, that leaves him “screwed.” The source does not further specify the dictionary’s implementation. In the prototype he was developing at the time of the conversation, he was spending substantial time laying a modular foundation before the project grew.

Repeated prototypes as technical learning

Robinson made Execute twice before producing the released third version. He built the first version in the engine transcribed as “GDAU” as one of his earliest projects. He liked the idea but concluded that he lacked the skills to finish it. The second version expanded into a world map with armies, robots and a parasite that could be launched to Earth, a lightning minigame that charged the robots, and a large skill tree in which each skill was a self-operating machine that needed resources. Resources could be switched between machines to activate different perks. He eventually concluded that the interconnected version was too much for him to organize and put it aside.

For the released version, he returned to the smaller first concept, retained selected ideas from the second, and removed much of the world map, the planned machines, and other options. The organizational limit was one reason for narrowing the design. The fuller relationship between these cuts, the game’s progression, and its focused player experience belongs with Focused Game Design, Progression, and Player Agency.

His other project, Grid Tweaker, also went through repeated construction. He had built it in Pico-8, made six versions, stopped the seventh version, and expected that he might remake it again. These iterations accompanied his willingness to leave a version unfinished when its idea, scope, skills, or organization did not support continuing it.

A novice developer’s perspective

Blake Gocey describes entering game development without a technical or game-development background. He tried, stopped, tried again, and later released the very small Wild Harmony on Steam. That release gave him experience with the publishing process and made him believe he could create a game if he invested the time.

While making it, Gocey still considered himself a novice. Another developer had described reaching a flow state in which most problems could be solved without consulting a resource; Gocey was at the opposite stage. Almost everything he wanted to implement required guidance from a forum, YouTube, or ChatGPT.

Gocey nevertheless found that spending time in an engine was enough to learn where things were and how to make elements appear. He identifies programming—especially deciding how two things should interact—as the hardest part of his experience. He treats art as a separate discipline that can also be challenging for different reasons.

Learning through Pico-8’s constraints

A self-contained environment

Robinson recommends Pico-8 as a place to dabble in game development because its tools are integrated. He lists a sprite editor, sound generator and digital audio workstation, code environment, and file-management system. A developer can launch and run a game within Pico-8. He also says it may be possible to post a game directly from it, but qualifies that claim: he is not certain and is only “pretty sure” a game can be exported and uploaded to what the transcript calls the “Pico-8 Exploratory thing.”

He describes Pico-8 as costing about $10 or being free through its education version. The low cost and self-contained toolset make it suitable for trying development without first assembling several separate tools.

Building fundamentals within limits

In Robinson’s account, Pico-8’s age and limited built-in functionality require the developer to code their own physics, rendering, and functions that a larger engine might provide as components. At the same time, its constraints limit the size of what can be made. This pairs with the common recommendation to begin with something small.

He does not necessarily recommend using Pico-8 to make a full game. He recommends cloning a game or building a small toy to learn. His example was a small incremental toy with crushers arranged in a grid: the player buys crushers, earns money from them, and keeps buying more. He learned the engine’s basics and then made that toy specifically to teach himself how those basics worked together. He also returns to Pico-8 to make small toys as a way to hone his development skills.

Moving to larger engines and external tools

After learning through Pico-8, Robinson found it easier to move into “GDAU.” The source repeatedly transcribes the engine’s name that way, and the uncertain name is retained rather than normalized. In the larger engine, he describes bringing in sprite work, learning a separate digital audio workstation and importing the resulting sound design, and learning Blender and importing that work. He also names Unity and Unreal while contrasting larger engines and their components with Pico-8’s constrained environment.

His recommendation is not specific to “GDAU.” He says Pico-8 would be useful before any larger engine. The video framed it as the fastest way to learn “GDAU” because that matched his own sequence and because the engine was trending at the time; the broader claimed benefit is learning game-development fundamentals in one small environment before also having to learn external production tools.
