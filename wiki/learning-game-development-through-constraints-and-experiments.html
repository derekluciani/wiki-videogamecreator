Learning Game Development Through Constraints and Experiments

Using constrained tools, small toys, analog prototypes, repeated attempts, and habitual exercises to develop practical game-making and creative skills.

A novice developer’s perspective

Blake Gocey describes entering game development without a technical or game-development background. He tried, stopped, tried again, and later released the very small Wild Harmony on Steam. That release gave him experience with the publishing process and made him believe he could create a game if he invested the time.

While making it, Gocey still considered himself a novice. Another developer had described reaching a flow state in which most problems could be solved without consulting a resource; Gocey was at the opposite stage. Almost everything he wanted to implement required guidance from a forum, YouTube, or ChatGPT.

Spending time in an engine nevertheless taught him where things were and how to make elements appear. He identifies programming—especially deciding how two things should interact—as the hardest part of his experience. He treats art as a separate discipline that can also be challenging for different reasons. The larger organizational questions that appear as those interactions multiply are covered in Code Organization, Performance, and Technical Constraints.

Learning through Pico-8’s constraints

A self-contained environment

Payne Robinson recommends Pico-8 as a place to dabble in game development because its tools are integrated. He lists a sprite editor, sound generator and digital audio workstation, code environment, and file-management system. A developer can launch and run a game within Pico-8.

Robinson also says it may be possible to post a game directly from Pico-8, but qualifies that claim: he is only “pretty sure” a game can be exported and uploaded to what the transcript calls the “Pico-8 Exploratory thing.” He describes Pico-8 as costing about $10 or being free through its education version. The low cost and self-contained toolset make it suitable for trying development without first assembling several separate tools.

Build fundamentals within limits

In Robinson’s account, Pico-8’s age and limited built-in functionality require the developer to code physics, rendering, and functions that a larger engine might supply as components. At the same time, its constraints limit the size of what can be made. This pairs with the common recommendation to begin with something small.

He does not necessarily recommend using Pico-8 to make a full game. He recommends cloning a game or building a small toy to learn. His example was an incremental toy with crushers arranged in a grid: the player buys crushers, earns money from them, and continues buying more. After learning the engine’s basics, he made the toy specifically to understand how those basics worked together. He also returns to Pico-8 to make small toys and hone his skills.

Move to larger engines and external tools

After learning through Pico-8, Robinson found it easier to move into the engine transcribed in the source as “GDAU.” In the larger engine, he describes bringing in sprite work, learning a separate digital audio workstation and importing its sound design, and learning Blender and importing that work. He also names Unity and Unreal while contrasting their components with Pico-8’s constrained environment.

His recommendation is not specific to “GDAU.” He says Pico-8 would be useful before any larger engine. The video framed it as the fastest way to learn “GDAU” because that matched his sequence and because the engine was trending at the time; the broader claimed benefit is learning fundamentals in one small environment before also learning external production tools.

Use analog prototypes to make designs unfamiliar

Raph Koster recommends thinking analog while exploring fundamental mechanics, including for digital action games. He describes digital play at a sufficiently small interval as a turn-based process whose turns expire about 60 times per second. Physical pieces can therefore expose rules that are obscured by continuous presentation.

His kit includes wooden squares and assorted shapes, binary-state pieces, stars, chips, hexes, circles, Go beads, and a hex mat. Asking whether a hex grid could sit beneath an FPS is one example of forcing a familiar game into an unfamiliar representation. Conventional and Uno decks similarly provoke different ideas through their suits, colors, and appearance. The mechanical transformations made possible by these materials appear in Game Mechanics, Variants, and Transformations.

Impose an apparently impossible constraint

Koster presents severe constraints as a deliberate creative tool. His analogy is gestural drawing: repeatedly drawing a still life in seven seconds prevents a familiar, leisurely approach. He reports producing a successful board-game design after limiting himself to selected pieces from his physical kit.

In a workshop exchange, audience members constrained a tactical RPG to fit within a cube and then made it nonviolent. Koster’s aim is to create a box that initially appears too small or contradictory, forcing the designer to search for mechanics that can operate inside it. This differs from Robinson’s capability constraints: Robinson begins with what he can implement well, while Koster deliberately invents restrictions to disrupt habitual solutions. Both use limits as design inputs.

Practice creativity habitually

Koster treats creativity as a skill that requires regular production rather than occasional inspiration. He cites a site transcribed as a game of.com, whose creator reportedly released one unique game each week, and working songwriters who write every day while discarding most results. Frequent output is practice even when little of it is retained.

He also recommends detailed mental visualization. His exercise is to picture a wheelbarrow and attempt to see its color, rust, dirt, straw, a brick in its corner, the shape of the handles, and a protruding screw. He qualifies the underlying claim with “supposedly”: people described as more creative are said to see greater detail in these internal movies. The exercise is intended to move perception beyond a generic familiar label.

Other exercises include reading broadly, mixing teams, moving a desk to a different location each week, and explaining an idea. Koster says explanation forces a designer to abstract a system, reduce it to its essentials, and examine its relationships. He reports that early MMO AI was partly inspired by MIT robots on roller skates as an example of useful material arriving from an unexpected field. The fuller relationship between varied inputs and idea generation appears in Finding, Testing, and Refining Game Ideas.

Retain failures and permit reasoned criticism

Koster advises keeping failed designs because moving them into a later context may make them workable. One tabletop prototype used colored round counters arranged in circles for a set-packing game. It was too fiddly to play and was shelved. Two years later, transferring the pattern to overlapping cards made the design viable. Koster said it was then expected to be published through entities transcribed as “Boing Boing and Cards Against Humanity,” with a Kickstarter later in 2014; the transcript does not clarify that publishing relationship.

He also rejects the criticism-free form of group brainstorming. He reports that it produces two ideas per person per hour, while permitting another participant to call an idea bad and explain why produces seven. These numbers are Koster’s attributed claim; the transcript does not identify the underlying research. His recommendation is to tolerate both failure and criticism rather than treating every initial idea as successful.

Working conditions for idea generation

Koster makes several broad claims about environments and physical state. In his account, a messy desk can create unusual accidental connections, while moderate noise and slight distraction can increase idea generation. He says the effective noise level had been measured but does not supply the decibel value; an unnamed website generated coffee-shop sound at that level.

He recommends exercise that allows the mind to wander rather than an activity in which a lapse of attention risks injury, standing for at least five minutes each hour, sufficient sleep, and handwritten rather than typed notes. He speculates that handwriting’s reported retention benefit may arise from its slower pace or different cognitive load and says he uses a tablet stylus. His 2014 recommendations also assume that smartphone motion sensors can track sleep and REM periods and time an alarm to natural waking.

Koster rejects the depressed-artist stereotype and says happiness and relaxation support creativity while high stress suppresses it. Warm showers, floating, exercise, and playing guitar are his examples. These environmental, sleep, and cognition statements are preserved as his January 2014 claims rather than generalized medical or psychological findings.

Learn the release process through repeated public games

Chris Zukowski argues that inexperienced developers need to put games in front of the public rather than learn entirely through private prototypes. His proposed cycle uses a weekend game jam, approximately one month of additional work, a free itch release, and three or four repetitions before selecting one result for fuller development. The selection method and its market tests appear in Finding, Testing, and Refining Game Ideas.

He expects early games to be poor and says a creator’s first five to ten may fail to connect. He cites Tyler Glaiel making approximately 56 games before later success, while noting that many of those projects were completed in roughly a week. These figures are rhetorical guidance and one developer’s example rather than thresholds every creator must meet. Zukowski advises studying the results, continuing after early failures, and avoiding bitter forum discussion that turns discouragement into a reason not to try again.

Zukowski distinguishes repeated prototypes from learning the complete release process. When an unpromising game can be wrapped within approximately another month, he recommends finishing and shipping it so the developer encounters packaging, launch, reviews, and the result of public response. He does not recommend adding several more years to a project that has repeatedly failed its public tests. Releasing some work, abandoning other work, and selecting the scale of further investment therefore develop related but different judgment.

Repeated attempts as learning

Robinson’s projects developed through repeated construction rather than one uninterrupted implementation. Execute was built twice before its released third version, and Grid Tweaker passed through six versions before he stopped a seventh and anticipated rebuilding it again.

These attempts let him preserve an idea while replacing work that exceeded his current skill, organization, or scope. The technical structure of those rebuilds appears in Code Organization, Performance, and Technical Constraints; their role in selecting ideas and deciding what to finish appears in Finding, Testing, and Refining Game Ideas.
