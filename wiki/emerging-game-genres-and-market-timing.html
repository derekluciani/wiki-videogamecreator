Emerging Game Genres and Market Timing

How active demand, undefined genre boundaries, production cost, maturation, and discovery channels can create temporary market openings.

A claimed 2025–2026 indie window

Thomas Brush describes 2024 and 2025 as brutal years for the game industry, marked by layoffs, publishers withdrawing funding, and cuts to his own indie project. Chris Zukowski accepts that contraction and attributes part of it to studios becoming too large, excessive funding reaching teams unprepared to use it, and indie games becoming bloated.

Zukowski nevertheless calls the period a “golden age” for a different kind of indie production. Based on his reading of Steam Next Fest results, genre charts, and interviews with dozens of developers, he sees several forms that small teams can make in approximately four to six months and that can spread rapidly on Steam. A developer does not necessarily need a large existing audience, though Zukowski says creative game design remains necessary.

He calls the alignment a “great conjunction”: several popular genres are simultaneously compatible with comparatively inexpensive and rapid production. This is not a promise that every small game will succeed. Zukowski describes failures as well as hits and treats all game development as risky; his claim is that the odds in these genres are currently better than in some alternatives.

In a later April 2026 conversation, Zukowski clarifies that “small” describes production structure rather than a fixed playtime. Replayable designs can provide effectively unlimited play, while much of their content may consist of numerical tuning maintained in spreadsheets instead of large quantities of handcrafted levels. His recommendation is not to compress a content-heavy form such as a Metroidvania into a short schedule.

Brush summarizes the moment as one in which the easiest games to make may also be the most profitable. Zukowski accepts that characterization only for the present window. The source does not establish how long it will last.

The later conversation adds Butterscotch Shenanigans as an attributed example. Zukowski says Crashlands 2 took roughly two or three years, left the team burned out, and performed acceptably rather than exceptionally. Their subsequent management game, How Many Dudes?, reportedly accumulated more wishlists in under nine months than Crashlands 2. He describes it as a management game with a hidden roguelike-deck-builder structure: the player receives random units, places them in an arena, watches them fight, and uses simple commands to send more. The game had not yet launched, so the comparison does not establish sales.

Low-cost conditions for mechanical experimentation

In a January 2014 talk, Raph Koster associates significant mechanical innovation with the casual and abstract ends of game development, particularly puzzle, indie, and mobile games. He attributes the period’s experimentation to low budgets, low costs of entry, and low costs of failure. He predicts that creativity will decrease as budgets rise because failure becomes more expensive.

Koster’s claim concerns conditions for trying new rule structures rather than proof of commercial demand. His techniques can also produce variants and extensions of existing genres without attempting to invent a wholly new game. These 2014 observations remain distinct from the 2025–2026 market window described elsewhere on this page; the sources do not establish a single continuous trend between them.

The practical transformations appear in Game Mechanics, Variants, and Transformations. Their financial uncertainty appears in Project Scope, Risk, and Sustainable Development.

Genres in the conjunction

The speakers’ lists vary during the conversations. The great conjunction is therefore a collection of active forms rather than a fixed taxonomy or a single audience.

Zukowski reports that approximately 18% of Steam gaming sessions use a controller and that the remainder use mouse and keyboard, without supplying a dataset or measurement period. He connects that context with games built around buttons, clicking, choices, familiar first-person controls, or simple cardinal movement rather than complex character handling.

His later list includes tightly restricted management and shop simulators, incrementals, choice-driven horror narratives, first-person horror, friend-slop games with simple or intentionally awkward controls, Vampire Survivors-likes, and chance-driven hybrids. Management and incremental production can concentrate much of its balance and content work in spreadsheet values. These properties make the forms comparatively manageable; they do not make development effortless.

Consumable and high-commitment audiences

Zukowski describes horror, narrative, incremental, and similar audiences as comparatively “consumable” or omnivorous. A player may finish a roughly hour-long horror game or a narrative game and immediately want another, leading some fans to buy dozens in a year. These are his broad observations rather than measured universal purchasing patterns.

He contrasts them with city builders, 4X games, and Factorio-like games. Players may invest deeply in one of those systems and buy only one or two such games annually. The genres can perform well and may not receive many releases, but they impose high thresholds for both development and player learning. His golden-age claim depends on the coincidence that some currently hungry audiences want forms that are also comparatively quick to produce.

Small-group cooperative games

Zukowski uses the term “friend slop” positively for small-group games in which approximately four or five friends usually pursue one shared goal. They may be cooperative or slightly competitive. He distinguishes them from large multiplayer games that need servers, lobbies, and a large active population, and from conventional party games such as Mario Party.

Peak, in which friends try to reach a summit, is the principal example. Earlier co-op horror games—including Lethal Company, R.E.P.O., and Phasmophobia—are presented as precedents before games such as Peak applied the social structure outside horror.

These games often retain visible jank: textures may clip, limbs may move strangely, and the handmade construction remains apparent. Zukowski values that visible human touch and argues that extensive polishing can remove it. He also reports that their moment-to-moment interactions are often kept simple so the game can facilitate conversation, jokes, and shared activity rather than consume every player’s attention.

Zukowski found relatively steady concurrent-player patterns for Phasmophobia and Lethal Company. He proposes that players may return in regular groups—for example, for a weekly session—but explicitly says he cannot establish that explanation from the data.

Idle and incremental games

The active incremental category includes Cookie Clicker-like games and combinations such as clicker city builders, civilization clickers, and black-hole incrementals. Zukowski considers the category unusually active and potentially undersupplied. He treats Xavier Nelson Jr.’s Click Holding as adjacent and believes the design space still contains possibilities.

Zukowski says some idle games have median playtimes around ten hours, longer than many conventional genres. He also notes that idle playtime is not directly comparable to active playtime, so the figure does not by itself establish equivalent engagement.

In a separate February 2026 account, Payne Robinson describes finding community lists containing hundreds or thousands of incremental games. He reports that web developers build them in JavaScript or React without learning a conventional game engine and that some creators even make them in PowerPoint. He also describes labels such as idle, incremental, active idle, and idle RPG as overlapping and poorly organized rather than a settled taxonomy.

Robinson gives a qualified answer to whether the category is development on “easy mode.” Button-driven interfaces can make incremental games comparatively approachable, and he believed demand was unusually active at the time. He nevertheless argues that strong reception still requires understanding genre expectations, presenting the game attractively, and adding a meaningful twist. He had seen games sell well while receiving weaker reviews, so market demand and player approval did not always move together.

Robinson reports that Execute sold within Steam’s top 20% and suggests that a three-month incremental could reach the top 30%. These are his interpretations of platform results, not verified thresholds or guarantees. He would not stake a larger project on copying a currently successful asteroid-destruction premise solely for revenue, though he allows that such imitation might serve as a student’s first experiment.

He connects Execute’s finite four-to-five-hour length with a perceived movement toward shorter Steam games, while noting that some of its negative reviews asked for a longer game. Current demand therefore did not establish one uniform preference. The fuller treatment of the game’s progression and finite structure appears in Focused Game Design, Progression, and Player Agency.

Horror as a genre and an overlay

Horror is described as viable for both single-player and cooperative games. It can also be placed over other structures, producing horror friend slop, horror shop simulators, and grim-dark casino games. Zukowski presents the suggestion that Brush could repurpose unused Twisted Towers assets for a horror incremental game as an illustrative combination, not as a tested prescription.

Casino and chance-driven hybrids

Zukowski identifies games combining slot machines or dice with grim-dark dungeons, fantasy framing, and RPG-like elements. The examples are CloverPit and Slots & Daggers. Brush observes that Slots & Daggers had approximately 1,500 very positive reviews after about a week. His suggestion that it may have been produced in roughly three months is explicitly speculative.

In the later conversation, Zukowski describes roguelite progression as one possible reason simple activities can support deeper repeat play. The structure can be layered over slots, carnival games, or an arcade-like activity such as Peglin’s underlying play. The full structural treatment appears in Game Mechanics, Variants, and Transformations.

Shop simulators and other clusters

Supermarket Simulator is presented as the breakout model for shop simulators, followed by developers reusing store assets and the basic format for additional shop games. Zukowski reports that two horror shop simulators performed strongly during Steam Next Fest.

The conversation also names simulation games, rage games, auto-battlers, a second generation of Vampire Survivors-likes, and emerging subgenres without established names. These receive less detail and do not necessarily share a player base or multiplayer structure.

The opening phase of a genre

Zukowski describes a “frothy” period in which a popular form has not yet established its boundaries and its audience wants more supply. During this phase:

  • developers can test variations rapidly;
  • players may tolerate inexpensive assets, jank, and limited polish;
  • low development cost can support a low consumer price, described as typically around $5;
  • early games help determine which mechanics will define the form.

Brush frames early entry as a gold rush: identify a newly popular genre and release into it before the opportunity closes. Zukowski objects to framing participation only as extraction. He compares the period with artists collectively exploring the boundaries of a new movement. In his view, early developers can influence a genre as well as benefit commercially from unmet demand.

Zukowski later makes the terminology explicit. A “golden age” describes favorable conditions that can benefit a broad group of creators and make sustainable creative work more attainable; a “gold rush” describes speculative entrants trying to exploit a finite opportunity. He intends the former. Lower barriers and active demand improve the odds but still require skill, persistence, cultural awareness, repeated releases, and basic marketing.

Following a trend or using it as a base for surprise

Robinson separately objects that a literal gold rush implies a finite deposit, while people may continue wanting games. He still expects the opening to change: audiences tire of repetition, and formulas that currently sell can lose effectiveness. His preferred response is to combine conventions into a different formula rather than copy the surface of a popular game. His next projects retained incremental influences while combining several genres, which also made their market category harder to communicate.

He later describes following trends, reskinning a working structure, or making a slight variation as legitimate when revenue is the primary objective, but not as his own general creative priority. He treats Medium Rare as a deliberate risk because he saw few currently successful comparable dungeon crawlers or turn-based games. Rather than convert it into what he considered a safer auto-battler, incremental game, or roguelike, he retained its first-person, turn-based extraction structure. Its initial wishlist response concerns visibility rather than proof that this risk will lead to completed commercial success; the full basis for the project choice appears in Finding, Testing, and Refining Game Ideas, while the creative and financial tradeoff appears in Project Scope, Risk, and Sustainable Development.

The unreleased title transcribed as A Meaningless Random Numbers supplies Robinson’s alternative within an active genre. It preserves incremental number growth and prestige while combining them with horror and anomalous imagery. He reports that it strongly outperformed other games in the most recent Steam Next Fest without supplying figures, but his prediction that it will succeed after release remained speculative. The fuller distinction between extending and breaking the presentation of a known form appears in Game Mechanics, Variants, and Transformations.

Both positions identify the same practical conditions: visible popularity, unclear boundaries, comparatively quick production, low costs, and fun taking priority over seamless polish. The relationship to short-project risk and sustainable production is central to the claimed opportunity.

Maturation raises the entry threshold

Vampire Survivors-likes

Zukowski uses the evolution of Vampire Survivors-likes as the main example. Near the genre’s 2021 breakout, a developer could use inexpensive assets, build a strong metagame, and produce a viable entry quickly. Creators experimented with foundational questions such as whether attacks should fire automatically, whether players should press buttons to shoot or roll, and whether movement should be the only direct input.

As the genre matured, players came to expect greater depth, polish, or recognizable intellectual property. Zukowski cites Deep Rock Galactic: Survivor and an announced Warhammer project as later examples backed by established properties. His position is not that the genre is dead, but that a quick three-month imitation is less likely to break through.

Mature Metroidvanias

Metroidvanias provide Zukowski’s contrasting example of a mature genre. He argues that expectations have been set by games such as Hollow Knight: Silksong and Ori and the Blind Forest. A new indie entry without comparable quality and depth may be dismissed immediately. The category remains possible, but its production threshold is much higher than that of an undersupplied emerging form.

Later polish

Zukowski predicts a possible sequence in which rapid experiments reveal what players want, conventions stabilize, and a later developer creates a highly polished version of the proven form. His MP3-player analogy is that many imperfect experiments preceded the iPod, after which Apple selected and refined an established direction. He speculates that, within four or five years, surviving idle games could require year-long production, high-end presentation, and substantially greater depth.

Reviving neglected genres

Brush asks whether a genre neglected for many years can create another opening even when a new game cannot match the old category’s most polished examples. Zukowski agrees that unmet demand can support revivals but describes this path as much riskier than entering a currently expanding genre.

He cites a developer working for three or four years on a Desert Strike-inspired game; the project’s title is unclear in the transcript. He also cites Paralives as benefiting from players’ perception that Electronic Arts has neglected The Sims. He does not know how many competing games either revived niche could support.

Zukowski compares genre decisions as relative risks rather than certain outcomes. The conjunction genres improve the odds without approaching a guaranteed or even 50/50 result; reviving an old genre may resemble choosing one number on a roulette wheel or buying a lottery ticket. He also maintains that established strategy, “crafty/buildy” simulation, and open-world survival-crafting games remain viable. A new market opportunity does not invalidate every earlier path.

Possible sources of demand

Players moving from Roblox to Steam

Zukowski relays a theory from a developer he interviewed: Roblox already contains many unusual small social games, and players raised on it may be aging into Steam. They may want a more mature environment while retaining the habit of using small digital games to spend time with friends. In the later account, the hypothetical high-school or college player has some disposable income and is willing to pay roughly $5 to play privately with friends rather than remain among much younger Roblox users. Steam’s growing population could therefore include a cohort already familiar with friend-slop-like play.

Zukowski finds the theory plausible but does not claim it is a complete or established explanation. His recommendation that game designers experience Roblox is based on the variety of game ideas there, not proof of the demographic theory.

From Banana to other idle games

Zukowski separately theorizes that speculative interest brought people to Banana, after which some discovered that they enjoyed idle play and sought other examples. He does not provide evidence connecting that event to the wider growth of incremental games. He likewise does not know why Vampire Survivors-likes expanded, though he proposes that adding roguelite elements may have helped the form evolve.

Immediate reaction or durable social activity

Brush proposes “TikTok games” as another description: games built around immediate gratification, social appeal, laughs, and quick emotional reactions. His snack metaphor asks whether the games are designed for prolonged, low-intensity consumption rather than the concentrated experience of a polished story game.

Zukowski rejects the comparison for small-group cooperative games. He characterizes TikTok as short-form and isolating, while games played with friends create social contact and may become recurring activities. The disagreement does not explain every conjunction genre because both speakers recognize that idle games and some horror games are single-player.

Streaming, visual appeal, and discovery

Brush suggests streaming may connect many active genres. Games that produce reactions, conversation, and laughter allow a streamer’s personality to carry part of the appeal. He contrasts this with older retail discovery, illustrated by seeing the cover of Alice: Madness Returns in a store.

Zukowski accepts that many conjunction games work well for streaming but rejects the claim that streaming controls all viable genres. Visually impressive games can still attract players through trailers, screenshots, TikTok, Reddit, and other social sharing without substantial streamer coverage.

Exo One is the visual-discovery example. Brush estimates, from its $17 price and roughly 3,000 very positive reviews, that it earned more than $1 million in revenue. This is an informal estimate rather than reported sales data. He contrasts its scale with conjunction games receiving tens or hundreds of thousands of reviews. Zukowski responds that a game need not match the largest viral hit to provide a sustainable career; visually led and streamer-led discovery can both remain viable.

Adding story, atmosphere, and polish

Brush worries that developers who value linear stories, atmosphere, and polish may be left behind by forms they do not personally enjoy. Zukowski does not advise abandoning those qualities. Because an emerging genre’s boundaries remain unsettled, creators can add narrative, environmental storytelling, thematic depth, or polish and potentially influence its direction.

David Wehle’s We Have a Shadow is presented as a model: it starts with a popular blue-collar-job simulation structure, compared with cleaning or power washing, and overlays story, themes, and atmosphere. Zukowski regards the established mechanical form as a foundation rather than a restriction.

In Peak, the Bing Bong object and its repeated presence in the environment prompt players to ask what it is and construct a narrative around it. Zukowski proposes that a cooperative game could similarly use warnings written in blood, environmental clues, implied relationships, or mysteries that friends discuss while playing. Story and social play are therefore treated as compatible rather than opposing directions.
