Game Performance and Technical Constraints

How runtime limits, batching, representational shortcuts, and implementation tradeoffs shape what a game can display while preserving its player-facing effect.

Protect the player-facing effect

Emerson identified the mass of visible objects in A Game About Feeding a Black Hole as part of the game’s central appeal, comparing it with the spectacle of the title transcribed as Norp. Optimization was therefore not simply a search for fewer objects. He first identified what the player needed to perceive, then changed the simulation and rendering beneath that effect.

His summary is that game development can rely on illusion: behavior need not be physically correct if it continues to look and feel right. Optimization choices were judged by whether they protected the visible scale and motion that made the game satisfying.

Replacing physics with simpler behavior

The objects originally used Godot physics so they could bunch together, collide, and rotate at different speeds. The physics calculations proved expensive and imposed hard limits on the number of objects.

Programmer Nick Glenn suggested replacing full physics with a simpler boids-like spacing system. Each object followed limited behavioral rules that moved it away from nearby objects rather than participating in a complete physical simulation. Emerson followed a guide to implement the initial version and reports that players did not complain about or apparently notice the change.

Simple rotational movement continued every frame. The more expensive spacing behavior was batched so that only approximately one-third of the objects received a boids-related velocity update during a frame. Emerson adjusted the batch size and frequency until the result felt acceptable.

Batch rendering with MultiMeshInstance

For the many pieces flowing into the black hole, Emerson used Godot’s MultiMeshInstance. In his description, thousands of ordinary sprites could require thousands of draw calls, while repeated instances of one texture could be sent as one batched rendering operation.

Instance identifiers allowed the game to change transforms and colors for individual pieces; Emerson also describes skew and shader changes as available capabilities, although he did not use all of them. The game moved each piece toward the center with a formula. As the number of pieces grew into the thousands, further optimization of this system became important.

Perceptual culling and representational scale

Later in the black-hole game, the output from smaller asteroids was deliberately reduced from roughly 15–20 visible pieces to one. At the same time, newly important planets and stars became physically larger on screen. Thornity proposed allowing those larger objects to draw attention away from the declining quantity of small pieces.

Emerson reports that almost no one noticed the reduction, although one player asked for a way to keep the larger quantity. The method preserved the impression of increasing scale while lowering the amount the game needed to display.

Execute used another representational shortcut. Robinson initially wanted a grid of 64×64 guillotine sprites to grow indefinitely and become progressively smaller. Godot would not continue scaling the grid as intended without a more elaborate canvas workaround, so he changed the presentation. A shader made a purchase appear to add guillotines behind an existing one, while some real instances were also added. Later, approximately 50 people could be rolled into one bundle, and a “mega guillotine” animation could represent roughly 100 executions in one chop.

Pooling, instantiation, and remaining limits

The black-hole game pooled effects and gradually instantiated needed objects while loading, avoiding most runtime creation. In Execute, most visible units were texture rectangles rather than spawned sprites.

The nuke sequence was an exception: workers spawned, collected iron, carried it to the nuke, and deposited it. Creating many of them together caused lag. Robinson had an object pool, but says pooling did not improve the sequence enough, and he chose not to investigate further. Object pooling is therefore presented as one technique rather than a complete solution to every performance problem.

Procedural visuals and layered UI

The black-hole game’s asteroids were procedurally generated with Godot Polygon2D and Line2D nodes, with AI assistance. Duplicated polygon and line layers represented health, while a clipped progress bar revealed the underlying color as damage accumulated. Emerson calls the construction “stupid” in an affectionate sense: the nodes were used in an unusual but effective way to create the intended appearance.

Construct visual assets through unconventional processes

Robinson uses Hylics to describe an accessible physical-to-digital process he calls rasterization. In his account, its creator made forms from clay, photographed them, cut the photographs out in Photoshop, imported them, lowered their resolution, forced their colors into a limited palette, and dithered them. He argues that the assets were constructed and transformed rather than conventionally drawn, and claims that most viewers could make the demonstrated clay shape. “Rasterization” is retained here as Robinson’s terminology and explanation.

Robinson says a related technique was used to make Medium Rare and the title transcribed as A Meaningless Random Numbers. In another visually demonstrated example whose exact game is unclear from the transcript, textures do not match and wood textures visibly repeat. He reads that appearance as a willingness to assemble available materials rather than attempt the consistency of a production such as Call of Duty.

His broader claim is that indie developers can create interesting and entertaining work by breaking visual and production conventions rather than competing directly on conventional polish. The corresponding design framework appears in Game Mechanics, Variants, and Transformations, while the intended effect on audience curiosity appears in Game Visibility, Trailers, and Creator Outreach.

A custom animation system and timer caps

Robinson did not use Godot AnimationPlayer nodes in Execute. His custom system uses a timer to advance through an array of individual PNGs, with a second timer used for some behavior between frames. He prefers separate PNGs to sprite sheets because he can directly replace an array of images and understand what is changing.

The preference carries a runtime limit. Robinson found Godot timers unreliable at very short intervals such as approximately 0.002, particularly in a browser or on older computers. Speed upgrades therefore received hard caps; without them, an apparent upgrade could make the game run more slowly rather than more quickly.

An API that supplied the current world population created a similar reliability tradeoff. Robinson disabled it and manually updated the population rather than allow an external call to disrupt play.

Bounded AI assistance rather than open-ended generation

Emerson finds open-ended “vibe coding” difficult. He uses Claude more successfully when he can provide an existing slow implementation and ask for optimization of that bounded mathematical or performance problem. He used it to improve the boids and MultiMesh work after first understanding their basic structure.

Both speakers remain cautious about opaque output. They describe spending substantial time understanding generated code, receiving over-engineered solutions, and encountering unwanted hard-coded strings. AI is presented as useful for specific optimization or mathematical tasks, not as a replacement for understanding the game’s architecture.
