Steam Demos, Festivals, Launches, and Commercial Lifecycle

How public tests, demo design, wishlists, festivals, launch surfaces, pricing, discounts, and post-launch decisions shape a Steam release.

Itch as a practice launch and proving ground

Payne Robinson and Emerson recommend treating an itch demo as a small launch even when the game remains rough. The page can be announced, supported with limited creator outreach, and posted to applicable communities such as Reddit. This rehearses promotional work before the Steam demo and full release while testing whether the game’s idea and presentation attract players.

Robinson reports that one Reddit post brought approximately 40,000 players to the first rough Execute demo. Emerson says A Game About Feeding a Black Hole continued receiving substantial itch play after its Steam release; the team planned to update that demo and redirect players toward the commercial game.

Chris Zukowski similarly treats a free, low-friction browser game as a commercial test. In his view, failure to attract players while the game is free is weak evidence that charging for it will improve demand. This is a market heuristic rather than proof. He recommends comparing itch performance with the benchmarks at howtomarketagame.com/benchmarks.

Testing an idea, practicing promotion, and learning to finish are related but distinct uses. The fuller treatment of public prototypes, including the danger of endless validation without completion, appears in Finding, Testing, and Refining Game Ideas.

An illustrative prototype-to-launch sequence

Zukowski supplies a hypothetical January-to-September sequence for a small game selected through public prototype testing. The dates illustrate an approximately nine-month production rather than prescribing universally optimal months.

  1. Create the Steam page but do not publish it immediately.
  2. Prepare a trailer.
  3. Use resources such as howtomarketagame.com/festivals to find online festivals accepting submissions.
  4. Offer a festival the announcement of the Steam version. In Zukowski’s view, an earlier itch release does not invalidate that announcement.
  5. Publish the Steam page when the announcement or festival begins.
  6. Offer the trailer to outlets such as IGN; a declined main-channel trailer might still be placed on a secondary channel such as GameTrailers.
  7. Approach Japanese social-media accounts when the game has visual or conceptual appeal suited to them.
  8. Release a quality Steam demo early—March in the example—and pair it with festival submissions and creator outreach.
  9. Enter the last Next Fest before launch—June in the example—with an updated and well-tested demo.
  10. Continue demo fixes and outreach before a September launch.

Zukowski says all of these actions can still produce almost no wishlists when the game itself does not attract players. He also advises against a publisher for this particular small-game scenario because of the added complexity; this is his attributed preference rather than a general prohibition.

Release a quality demo early

Zukowski recommends releasing the demo as early as possible without sacrificing acceptable quality. Because he does not offer a fixed quality threshold, he suggests first giving a private build to an experienced developer who has shipped games and acting on useful feedback.

An early public demo expands the test population. Players may reveal unsupported hardware, edge-case failures, confusing levels, or an overtuned boss that internal testing missed. Fixing those issues before Next Fest or major creator coverage improves the build encountered during the largest attention windows.

Early availability also accommodates the slow schedule of creator coverage. A creator may not publish for weeks or months, and one successful video may lead another creator to cover the game later. Before Next Fest, a developer can return to creators who previously liked the demo, explain what has changed, and invite them to cover the updated version. Zukowski also reports that simply having a good demo available appears to accumulate wishlists even when he cannot identify the precise source of each addition. The outreach practices supporting this process appear in Game Visibility, Trailers, and Creator Outreach.

Design the demo as a distinct experience

Zukowski advises against treating the demo as an unchanged first 20 minutes or imposing a simple timer on the opening. He recommends cutting to the strongest material and making the first ten minutes particularly engaging.

  • Remove lore and backstory that delay interaction.
  • Remove tutorial steps for conventions the genre’s audience already understands, while retaining the information actually needed.
  • Accelerate progression so the demo reaches representative rewards sooner than the full game.
  • For a role-playing game, consider supplying gear and a strong encounter rather than beginning with a long period of town dialogue.

Incremental interfaces can be comparatively self-tutorializing when they begin with one obvious button and expose further actions through play. The larger principle is to demonstrate the game’s appeal rather than require the player to wait for it.

Zukowski’s technical preference is to separate code from content so demo-specific content and pacing can be toggled without maintaining two independent games. He acknowledges that this architecture is easier to recommend than implement. The technical tradeoff appears in Code Organization, Performance, and Technical Constraints.

Build wishlists before Next Fest

Zukowski reports that, in his survey data, the total number of wishlists entering Next Fest correlates more strongly with festival performance than recent wishlist velocity. Velocity also correlates with performance, and teams with substantial totals may simultaneously have appealing games, effective marketing, and continuing momentum. He therefore warns that correlation does not establish which factor caused the result.

Games do not choose between entering with a large total and entering with velocity; strong games may have both. Zukowski warns that anecdotes about games succeeding with few wishlists and sudden momentum create survivorship bias because low-wishlist games whose momentum produced little are less visible. He identifies one exceptional incremental as a game in a particularly active genre.

His benchmark data reportedly predicts ranges of Next Fest wishlist gains from the entering total. The transcript does not provide the dataset or methodology. His practical recommendation is to make a good demo available early enough to gather wishlists, creator coverage, and feedback before the festival rather than treating Next Fest as the beginning of discovery.

Changing Next Fest distribution

Zukowski says he has collected developer survey data across approximately five or six years of Next Fests. In his account, June 2024 distributed an unusually large number of impressions, particularly to top-tier games. October 2024 then contained approximately 3,000 games and produced an unusually severe drop in impressions after Valve apparently changed a machine-learning recommendation system.

He infers that Valve reduced and later retuned distribution; Valve does not confirm that account in the source. One transcript sentence about the duration of algorithmic visibility is garbled and cannot establish an exact rule.

Butterscotch Shenanigans reportedly supplied Zukowski with data for Crashlands 2 and How Many Dudes?. Crashlands 2 received approximately ten times as many impressions in its Next Fest, yet each game reportedly gained about 15,000 wishlists. How Many Dudes? later reached Steam’s top-50 demos, which its developers described as producing an especially large spike.

Zukowski interprets equivalent wishlist totals from fewer impressions as potentially more effective targeting: later distribution may have acted like a narrow beam aimed at interested players rather than broad exposure to less interested viewers. This is his interpretation of the supplied figures, not a confirmed explanation of Steam’s algorithm.

Execute’s reported wishlist path

Approximately two weeks before launch, Robinson had about 4,000 wishlists. He said reaching that figure had taken approximately two or three months and was difficult, despite substantial outreach to about 100 creators.

Wishlist sources before and during Next Fest

  • Incremental-strategy YouTuber Orbital Potato covered the game in a video that had approximately 70,000 views at the time Robinson discussed it.
  • A smaller creator made another video with approximately 1,000 views.
  • Robinson also identified his development logs, a Reddit post, and the availability of the itch demo as contributing sources.
  • Steam Next Fest itself produced approximately 1,400 wishlists.

The source’s breakdown is internally inconsistent and remains unresolved. Robinson said he had approximately 2,000—or perhaps fewer than 2,000—wishlists before Next Fest and connected that total to Orbital Potato. He then said the two creator videos produced approximately 1,800 between them, while perhaps 300–400 from a development log also contributed to that 1,800. He subsequently said the creator coverage, development log, Reddit post, and itch demo together gave him “about 800.” These statements do not produce one consistent subtotal, and the transcript does not establish which number or grouping was intended.

Community estimates and Popular Upcoming

When Robinson asked the incremental-development channel whether launching with only about 4,000 wishlists left the game in trouble, the responses were pessimistic. One developer advised delaying the launch. Robinson reports being told that approximately 5,000–6,000 wishlists were needed to reach Steam’s Popular Upcoming page and that appearing there could essentially double the total. This was a community estimate reported in the conversation, not an official Steam threshold or a demonstrated guarantee.

Thornity offered a qualified counterexample: a game had launched with only about 3,000 wishlists, but Robinson recalled the reassurance as depending on being something like a “one-in-a-million hit.” The exchange increased rather than removed Robinson’s concern.

Another developer then shared a spreadsheet containing 500 content-creator names. Robinson immediately sent keys broadly across that list and attributed approximately 2,000 additional wishlists to the effort. That moved the reported total from roughly 4,000 to roughly 6,000. Execute reached Popular Upcoming and had approximately 9,000–10,000 wishlists by launch day.

Demo outreach before Next Fest

Robinson considered his failure to conduct demo outreach before Next Fest a major mistake. By comparison, he said A Game About Feeding a Black Hole paired its demo with creator outreach, generating a content cycle before Next Fest and another during the festival. In his account, the resulting wishlists and visibility continued compounding after the event.

His estimates of the missed opportunity were explicitly counterfactual. He speculated that a demo-outreach cycle might have brought Execute to approximately 6,000, 8,000, or perhaps 10,000 wishlists at that stage, allowed a launch with approximately 15,000–20,000, and perhaps doubled sales. These figures describe what Robinson believed might have happened; the conversation does not resolve or verify them.

Popular Upcoming, New & Trending, and calendar timing

Robinson distinguished pre-launch placement on Popular Upcoming from post-launch time on New & Trending. He reported launching during the Steam winter sale. When the sale began, sale games entered New & Trending and truncated the launch’s time on that list.

He treated the collision as luck when the team did not know enough to anticipate it, but as reusable knowledge afterward: with that experience, they would avoid planning the same way again. Other games releasing at the same time can similarly consume attention, making release timing one part of launch preparation rather than an entirely controllable condition.

Use creator attention while launch surfaces are available

Zukowski cites Terminus: Zombie Survivors as a game that reportedly launched only in Korean, remained quiet for approximately six months, and grew after an English translation allowed streamers and creators to find it. He treats that recovery as evidence that a good game can be discovered late, but also as evidence of opportunity cost.

He frames Steam’s 30% revenue share as payment in part for access to surfaces such as Popular Upcoming, New & Trending, and the launch Discovery Queue. Creator coverage and wishlists assembled beforehand may help a game qualify while those launch opportunities remain available. Waiting until after release can forfeit part of that concentrated period even if creators eventually discover the game. This is Zukowski’s interpretation of the platform exchange rather than Valve’s stated allocation of the fee.

Price from comparable games

Zukowski begins by saying he does not know the correct price for an individual game. His proposed comparative method is to list games similar in genre, visual quality, and depth, record their prices, cross-reference their reviews and player sentiment, calculate the average, and consider pricing approximately 20% above it.

He argues that indie games sell heavily during discounts and are generally priced too low. He says his charts show nominal indie prices declining while AAA prices rise, without presenting the charts or accounting for inflation in this conversation. His claim that a game may be underpriced if nobody complains about its price is a provocative supply-and-demand interpretation, not a demonstrated pricing law.

Post-launch triage

Zukowski reports analyzing the full set of games released during 2024 because later cohorts had not accumulated enough post-launch history. He says only 25 of approximately 20,000 games recovered after what he defines here as a failed launch: receiving fewer than 150 reviews during the first month. He summarizes this as approximately a one-in-1,000 chance of later success.

The transcript does not provide the dataset, methodology, or threshold used to define the later success of those 25 games. The figures should therefore be read as Zukowski’s reported analysis rather than an independently verifiable platform rule.

For a game below the 150-review threshold, he recommends fixing severe bugs to protect the developer’s reputation, using Steam’s pricing tools to schedule future discounts, and otherwise moving quickly to the next project. He says discounts can be scheduled around Steam’s cooldown periods and approximately a year ahead; current platform rules may differ.

Zukowski says most of the 25 recoveries were good games whose developers had not conducted creator outreach before launch. Even then, he believes they lost potential performance by missing the initial platform surfaces. His reference to approximately 7,000 pre-launch wishlists is a target used in this context rather than an official guarantee.

His forceful statement that a failed game cannot be fixed means that recovery is extremely rare in his dataset, not that later success is literally impossible. The decision to stop investing must also be understood alongside the learning value of completing some early projects and the broader management of limited development time in Project Scope, Risk, and Sustainable Development.
