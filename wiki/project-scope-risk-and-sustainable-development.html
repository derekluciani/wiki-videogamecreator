Project Scope, Risk, and Sustainable Development

How project duration, financial exposure, creative ambition, practical planning, and definitions of success shape sustainable game development.

Short projects as risk and feedback management

Chris Zukowski describes four-to-six-month games as a lower-risk alternative to multi-year development. Shorter production reduces the money and time committed before market response is visible, permits more attempts, and can provide a break from a longer project. He compares a long production cycle to waiting years for a photograph to be developed before discovering that it did not turn out.

Zukowski reports interviewing developers who paused four-year “dream games” to make shorter games. Some of those shorter projects performed better than the longer projects appeared likely to perform, and some developers did not return to the original work. These are attributed anecdotes rather than a quantified success rate. Short production also does not guarantee success: Zukowski says his charts include great-conjunction games that failed as well as enormous hits.

The commercial circumstances discussed here are tied to the source’s claimed 2025–2026 window for inexpensive games in active, emerging genres. The scope recommendation is therefore not presented as independent of genre and market timing.

Innovation as creative and financial risk

In a January 2014 talk, Raph Koster cautions that innovation does not imply success and often produces the opposite. He describes significant mechanical innovation as expensive and highly risky, and says even disciplined creative practice will probably fail more often than it succeeds. His position qualifies rather than replaces the other sources’ treatment of distinctiveness, ambition, and commercial opportunity.

Koster says creative leaders are often perceived as worse leaders than people who follow established practices, and that the person who invents a new game is often not the person who receives its largest financial return. He therefore does not present mechanical innovation as a reliable route for someone motivated primarily by predictable income.

His reasons to proceed include enjoyment, opening other opportunities, and leaving a mark. He also allows two business benefits: successful execution can establish a “blue ocean,” and a creator’s distinctive form of creativity can build a recognizable brand. His accompanying statement that “marketing as we know it is dead” is retained as a broad 2014 assertion, not a current conclusion. The design practices involved appear in Game Mechanics, Variants, and Transformations, while the production conditions he associates with experimentation appear in Emerging Game Genres and Market Timing.

In an August 2026 account, Robinson advances a different attributed theory: unfamiliarity can itself attract attention. He knowingly treats Medium Rare as riskier than an auto-battling incremental or roguelike because he saw few currently successful comparable dungeon crawlers or turn-based games. He nevertheless accepts that mixing favorite influences can create an overscoped mess. His claimed protection was prior experience making and shipping several small games, which helped him keep this hybrid small; manageable scope was part of why he chose it. The full basis for that project choice appears in Finding, Testing, and Refining Game Ideas.

Robinson interpreted the early wishlist response to Medium Rare’s trailer as a response to its unusual concept and presentation. The reported wishlist and traffic details appear in Game Visibility, Steam Launches, and Creator Outreach. Those early visibility results do not establish release sales or long-term performance. His position therefore remains beside Koster’s warning rather than resolving whether innovation improves commercial odds.

A side project that became commercially significant

Payne Robinson paused the larger 3D project Grid Tweaker while it was draining him during a period of personal difficulty and demanding school work. He intended Execute to be a roughly two-month side project released casually on itch, after which he would return to the larger game. Positive itch reception and interest in his YouTube videos led him to bring it to Steam, partly to practice the launch process before releasing Grid Tweaker.

About five calendar months passed from starting Execute to launching it. Robinson estimates that the actual labor was equivalent to approximately eight 40-hour weeks, while acknowledging that this estimate may be low. Emerson described an earlier January 2026 snapshot as approximately 96% positive across 250 Steam reviews. At the time of the February 2026 interview, the game had sold around 20,000 copies; Blake Gocey reported that approximately 95% of its Steam reviews were positive. Based on that reception, Robinson believed it might become a cult classic within the incremental genre; this was his expectation rather than an established status.

Robinson initially interpreted those sales as a failure because podcasts, industry veterans, and successful peers had accustomed him to thresholds of 50,000 or 100,000 copies. Gocey argues that success must instead be evaluated relative to its inputs: the result can be exceptional for spare-time work and inadequate for a project that consumed two years of full-time development.

Before release, Robinson expected approximately $10,000, or roughly 4,000 sales, and would have considered that successful. The actual result paid his student loans and was “year-changing” but, in his assessment, not permanently life-changing if he stopped making games. He and a friend projected that it might continue producing at least approximately $1,000 per month, possibly for two years; this was a forecast rather than an observed long-term result.

Finishing, discarding, and rebuilding projects

Robinson explicitly disagrees with the advice that every game must be finished. During approximately ten years of music production, making thousands of songs taught him to distinguish ideas worth completing, mixing, and mastering from ideas that were unlikely to become satisfying finished work. He cautions that this judgment develops more slowly in games: a musical attempt may take a day, while several years without publishing a game can make a developer feel broken or unsuccessful.

Execute was built twice before its released third version. Robinson lacked the skill to complete the first. The second expanded the execution premise with a world map, robot armies, a parasite, lightning collection, multiple machines, modular perks, and interconnected resources. He shelved it because he could not organize its complexity, then returned to the first version’s focus while retaining selected ideas. Grid Tweaker had similarly passed through six or seven versions, and he anticipated rebuilding it again.

Some cuts were design judgments and others were production constraints. Robinson believed he had time to make only one progression curve satisfying, so additional machines and options risked weakening the player experience. The fuller treatment of this focus and progression design appears in Focused Game Design, Progression, and Player Agency; the relationship between repeated experiments and technical organization appears in Code Organization, Performance, and Technical Constraints.

Validate early without making every project a prototype

Robinson and Emerson treat itch releases as low-friction tests that can limit commitment before market response is visible. Robinson reports that one Reddit post brought approximately 40,000 players to an early, rough Execute demo. He later abandoned Grid Tweaker because it did not attract enough players and because he did not believe he could execute it at the required quality.

Emerson preserves the opposing risk: a developer can remain in “prototyping hell,” repeatedly seeking a validated concept without developing the separate skills required to finish, package, and release a complete product. The speakers do not resolve this tension into a requirement to finish or abandon every test. The full idea-validation method appears in Finding, Testing, and Refining Game Ideas.

Minimum viable professionalism

Robinson praises A Game About Feeding a Black Hole for its modes, settings, interface, skill tree, and studio-like presentation. He argues that first-time developers should not conclude that their own game requires that entire feature set before it can be finished. Multiple languages, extensive accessibility settings, several modes, controller support, and other desirable refinements can exceed a novice’s technical or organizational capacity.

Emerson says controller support, localization, the mode selector, and similar additions came near the end of his project, after the central game was fun. His previous experience allowed him to add them comparatively quickly. Execute, by contrast, consisted primarily of buttons and static animations and had only enough supporting polish to “squeak by,” in Robinson’s assessment; he believes its gameplay carried the remainder.

This is not an argument against polish or accessibility. It is a warning against treating every professional feature as a prerequisite for a novice’s first completion. Emerson adds that a game can remain successful despite substantial flaws when its central experience works, citing his enjoyment of Valheim despite disliking its item-durability system.

The costs of a long project

Thomas Brush describes Twisted Towers as a story-driven, immersive, moody project approaching double-A scale. At the time of the November 2025 conversation, he had spent roughly four years and, by his estimate, more than $500,000 on it. He reports working 14–16-hour days, repeatedly extending the completion date, neglecting basic self-care, and experiencing strain in his marriage and in his responsibilities as a parent and friend.

Financially, Brush compares the experience to playing one poker hand for four years while knowing that he will eventually have to go all-in. The delayed release creates persistent worry about whether the game will recover its investment. He had already begun recommending smaller games in response both to market trends and to his own burnout.

Brush nevertheless distinguishes production hardship from a wrong creative choice. Large story games, atmosphere, depth, and polish are central to what he wants to make. He says an ambitious project can satisfy a personal or “spiritual” goal that a smaller project may not, even when the process leaves him exhausted.

Maximum outcomes and sustainable outcomes

The 10x question

Brush raises the argument from 10x Is Easier Than 2x: aiming for an exceptional result may be a better gamble than making smaller work with limited margins. He also asks whether a developer might begin with 1x or 2x outcomes and progressively undertake larger projects. In his view, not every developer should continually release small games; some people are suited to deep, highly polished projects sold at $50–$60.

Zukowski rejects 10x as a general game-development objective. He associates it with venture-capital expectations and argues that exceptional indie successes shifted creators’ target from modest income toward results on the order of $10 million. In his account, those expectations encourage bigger projects, greater risk, and dependence on publishers.

Repeated modest returns

Zukowski contrasts those expectations with the Flash-game period, when he recalls sponsorship and advertising returns of approximately $10,000–$20,000 per game. A creator could calibrate scope to that ceiling and make several games in a year rather than spend four years pursuing one exceptional outcome.

His preferred measure of success is whether a developer can continue making games sustainably, maintain a middle-income life, support a family, and avoid measures such as mortgaging a home. A game represented by only a small mark beside a much larger hit on a chart may still have earned several hundred thousand dollars and produced a viable income. The figures are examples from his account; the underlying games and revenue records are not provided in the source.

The documentary 20 Feet from Stardom supplies Zukowski’s analogy for a creative career without maximum fame. Backup singers and session musicians can contribute to major works, earn a comfortable living, and value the work without becoming the most visible artist. He applies that model to developers who make a sustainable living without producing the market’s largest game.

Creative fulfillment at different scales

Brush cautions that small projects are not intrinsically enjoyable. A developer drawn to an ambitious vision may be less happy making smaller games. He compares changing from a large game to a small one with changing from paintball guns to squirt guns: both are toys, but making them is meaningfully different to him.

Zukowski answers that even a different kind of game remains creative work capable of entertaining players. He believes some developers misidentify completion of a particular dream concept as the source of happiness when enthusiastic player response may be what they actually seek. As an example, he describes a developer whose earlier videos chronicled unsuccessful projects and whose later videos showed greater happiness while developing an unreleased black-hole game that was accumulating many wishlists. The developer’s and game’s names are unclear in the transcript.

The speakers do not resolve the preference question. Brush may test smaller games but maintains that rapid, iterative work and long, polished work each have a place. Zukowski believes many exhausted developers would benefit from pausing a long project and trying a shorter one.

Creative commitment and business development

Before releasing Execute, Robinson would have placed himself almost entirely on the artistic or development side of a proposed divide between art and business. After the launch, while teaching, working multiple jobs, developing games, and expecting a child, he became more interested in business development and financial stability. He wanted enough income to choose his work and sometimes avoid conventional employment, not to maximize revenue at any creative cost.

His stated creative boundary is to make something he would have wanted to play as a child. He remains willing to cut features or discard a project that is not good enough rather than release solely for income. At the same time, he enjoys learning launch strategy and compares business development with learning the rules, hazards, and walkthrough of a game. Gocey characterizes the artist-versus-business choice as false in Robinson’s case: Robinson chooses work he values and then tries to improve its chance of supporting him.

The launch moved full-time development from a remote possibility to a plausible goal, although Robinson believed the game had fallen just short of enabling him to leave his job. He described YouTube as teaching, marketing, networking, and a financial backbone in case a game fails. The channel and games together could support his goal of teaching subjects he chooses while continuing to develop games. The fuller treatment of YouTube’s role in discovery appears in Game Visibility, Steam Launches, and Creator Outreach.

Robinson distinguishes commercial activity from allowing trends or monetization to redirect all of the work. He considers following active genres and slightly changing a proven structure legitimate when revenue is the primary objective, but says that is not why he makes games. Execute was chosen because he liked incremental games and wanted a small project, not merely because incrementals were popular; his broader preference is work he wants to play and can be proud of. His Roblox experience provides one caution: adults designing for children they did not understand relied on copying what that audience already played. He accepts limited sponsorship and monetization but objects to reorganizing an entire creative practice around advertisements, reviews, free products, or trends that override the direction desired by the people making the work.

Make scope visible

After trying Trello, stories, and other software-development planning methods, Emerson prefers a list of approximately three to five major tasks. A separate “maybe someday” list functions largely as a discard area. He identifies more work after completing the short active list rather than keeping every possible task in front of him.

Robinson uses handwritten notes and plans to keep the next project’s core ideas visibly attached to his computer. His model is Execute’s initial boundary of buttons and static animations. The intended one currency grew to roughly ten, but the technical boundary still constrained what the project could become.

Working continuously with artist Chew creates a new source of scope pressure. Chew thinks through drawing and converts Robinson’s explanations into convincing game-screen-like illustrations. Seeing an idea rendered attractively makes Robinson want to add it, so he intends to test each proposed addition against the written core rather than its immediate visual appeal.

A teacher friend serving as project manager requires Robinson to document decisions, discuss them, and set deadlines. Deadlines let available weekly hours be compared with a feature’s cost. Robinson also identifies a limit to this method: he may not know what belongs until the game is playable, while the time required to reach a playable version may itself be uncertain.

Manage energy outside the project

Emerson uses yoga, exercise, therapy, and regular routines to manage launch anxiety and maintain his ability to work and participate in relationships. Before a December release, he exercised or practiced yoga approximately five or six times in one week to discharge nervous energy. He says he works best when waking around 6:00 a.m. and sleeping around 9:00 p.m., while noting that Minnesota winters make the schedule harder. Once the tested build was complete, he avoided unnecessary late changes that could introduce bugs or disturb balance; some known bugs had effectively become part of the validated experience.

Robinson combines development with teaching and an after-school job. Because those jobs leave him with decision fatigue, he journals and works through code on paper while mentally fresh, then lets his tired evening self follow the earlier instructions. When a difficult problem remains, he documents it for a later session. Weekends can provide approximately four-hour coding periods. His partner prepares the week’s meals on Sundays, while a home pull-up and dip bar provides a maintenance-level substitute after winter interrupted his previous four-times-weekly gym attendance.

Neither speaker presents a perfect routine. Exercise, external support, advance planning, and deliberate management of mentally demanding work are described as parts of sustaining development alongside the rest of life.

Boundaries around a conventional job

As a teacher, Robinson says his contract prevents him from advertising his game to students. He allows them to find publicly available material independently but does not provide links, and he removes current students who enter his development Discord. Because Execute concerns eliminating the world’s population, he also recognizes a potential employment risk beyond the ordinary difficulty of promoting creative work while holding another job.

Suffering and artistic identity

Suffering is not proof of artistic value

Zukowski rejects the idea that an artist must suffer or that visible anguish confers legitimacy. He argues that creative work can be excellent and meaningful while its makers enjoy the process. He specifically objects to treating suffering as valor.

He believes Indie Game: The Movie contributed to a production culture in which impressionable developers saw successful creators in crisis and inferred that sacrifice and distress were necessary parts of making art. His concern is that newcomers may select projects and adopt harmful behavior to reproduce an image of the serious artist even though suffering has no demonstrated correlation with quality.

Hardship can accompany chosen ambition

Brush agrees that suffering neither proves artistic status nor guarantees a great game. His caveat is that prolonged difficulty can accompany the intense detail required by an ambitious project, and misery alone does not prove that the project is wrong. He cites Judas, described in the conversation as having been in development under Ken Levine for about ten years, while acknowledging that its outcome was not yet known.

Brush argues that there can be a time for grind, persistence, and polish as well as a time for iteration and rapid release. Zukowski accepts that great work can be made under difficult circumstances but continues to reject hardship as a prerequisite developers should imitate.

The common ground is narrower than either position alone: developers can choose different ambitions, but suffering should not define game development. Joy and a sustainable process are worth attempting, while neither ease nor hardship by itself determines whether a project is worthwhile.
