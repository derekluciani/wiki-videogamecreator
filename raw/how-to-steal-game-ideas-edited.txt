Article title: How to Steal Game Ideas (Without Copying)
Author/Speaker: Payne Robinson
Date: 2026/02
Source Url: https://youtu.be/dXuhtXnv6qU?si=AB4V3_2RykX0xRJO

This is a nuanced take on strategically stealing ideas from other games because I always tell people to play more games. Here's my take. Let's start with Jonas Tyroller. Much of this is based on his fantasy theory. If you've never heard it, he says that when you come up with game ideas, you should start with the fantasy. The reason is that if you want to make money from games, you need to think about the audience. If you don't care about making money, that's fine. I'm not saying you need to think about how to make money. If you care most about making money, you should just clone successful games. I'm approaching this from the middle: if you want to be an artist who makes enough money to pay the bills, you need to think about your audience so you can build a stable income while creating a fantasy you like. Jonas's fantasy theory lets you have both: a fantasy you find interesting and an audience for it. For instance, the fantasy of being a vampire bank robber. That's compelling: vampires and bank robbery are both cool. That fantasy is easy to sell: if you say, "Hey, I'm making a game about being a vampire bank robber." People instantly understand it and say, "Oh, cool. That sounds like a cool fantasy I want to be a part of." You have a clear foundation. Being a vampire bank robber could become a turn-based strategy game. It could also be a management, first-person, stealth, or action game. Many people start with mechanics instead of a fantasy, which leads them here. This example is intentionally simple. If we start with mechanics, you could say, "I'm gonna make a cute bunny platformer." There is a little fantasy there—some people like cute bunnies—but it's generic. I'm going to make a platformer. Compare that with the fantasy: you want to make a game where the player feels like the cutest bunny alive. That could be a visual novel. It could be a Roblox-style dress-up game where you dress different bunnies. Starting with a cute bunny platformer—or with mechanics in general—locks you into a specific approach. Starting with the fantasy keeps the audience in mind, then lets you shape the game around something you think people will respond to. The size of a fantasy may not matter to you, but it is worth considering. Every fantasy has a potential market size. Here are some examples. Cheating at poker to win big is the fantasy that Balatro fulfills. It is a huge fantasy. Many people have wished they could cheat at cards or in a casino and win. Millions of people share that fantasy, which is part of why Balatro was exciting. That is the fantasy it sells: cheating at poker. That is what drew me in: I've always wanted to cheat at poker and win. The entire game is built around doing that deliberately. It is a compelling fantasy that many people share. Smaller fantasy. I don't care about mass appeal. None of this means, "Make your game appeal to everyone." People ask why their game did not sell even though it was good. You need to choose a fantasy that people actually respond to if you want them to play your game. My fantasy is ending the world in a gory, horrific way. That is a smaller audience, but people still respond to the fantasy. If I wanted a larger audience, I would choose a broader fantasy, but I like making enough money to keep making games. If audience or fantasy size matters to you, consider this; if not, don't worry about it. Just choose something that resonates with someone, or nobody will buy it. Recently, I watched the GameDev Dream podcast, where Jonas Tyroller explained his fantasy theory in a way that helped me understand it: you might ask, "What is a fantasy?" Thinking in terms of fantasies puts the question in your hands. You stand in the developer's shoes and ask, "How do I sell this world I'm creating?" You might decide, "I'm creating a world where you are a toilet shooting people with poop. How do I sell that fantasy?" I did not find that way of thinking about fantasy useful until he said, "Think of fantasies as cravings people have when looking for games. People play video games to fulfill cravings. When they see a game that fulfills one, the fantasy has met that craving." This puts you in other people's shoes: instead of asking how to invent and sell a fantasy, you ask what people are craving. Because I play many video games, I can often rely on my own perspective. I ask myself what I am craving and what games I have not seen made. That was part of why I made Execute: I liked Plague Inc. but wanted something more violent. I missed the awful, dark, strange games of the early 2000s. That was what I craved, and I figured others must crave it too. Sure enough, 30,000 people did. Think about your own cravings, especially if you are a gamer; they can reveal these fantasies. A case study may help illustrate this with a specific fantasy. This is the stealing part. If you are wondering when we will get to stealing, here is how. I am making a game that uses this fantasy. Here are some of the things I considered. The fantasy is: Control giant machinery. You might ask, "What does that mean?" If you have seen construction machines or their toy versions, you understand their appeal. You may not care about digging; maybe you think it is for boys who want to become construction workers. That is fine. People enjoy big machines, which is why this toy exists and is popular. The existence of toys based on it shows that people like this fantasy. This game is about controlling a giant planetary artillery gun from a cockpit full of levers and gadgets. You sit inside it as you would inside a real machine, controlling buttons, reading radar, and operating the machinery. It is a fantastical version of that experience: you are in the future operating a machine that does not exist in real life. This is the fantasy of controlling giant machinery. What do we steal? You should not clone these games or say, "Armored Core 6: I'm going to make a mech game where you do this exact same thing." This is where people make a mistake. They say, "I'm going to clone this idea that clearly worked. Farming Simulator: I'm going to make my own version." You can see that PVKK is influenced by games like Farming Simulator. But they evolved the idea and made up their own machine. Here is another example. I am working on a game called Grid Tweaker, where you control a computer that will eventually have a large window looking out like PVKK. You drop huge objects onto a power grid and use it to make money. I do not know when it will come out. This is not an advertisement; it shows how to warp a fantasy and take inspiration from it. Do not clone the game, steal its art, or use its IP. Steal its solutions to friction. In PVKK and Farming Simulator, there is friction in understanding how the machines work; in real life, they are not always intuitive. In an interview with Jonas Tyroller, the head of BIP and Bits said, "We want to make everything in here extremely intuitive so that you understand exactly what things do." I have never played the game, but if you crank this wheel, it probably turns the artillery cannon. A big red button—whether bell or button—probably fires it. Steal that perspective and apply the design philosophy to your own game. If you make a giant-machine game, make it intuitive. Next: upgrade structures. Many incremental games use them. If you are making an incremental game, you might steal the skill-tree structure. I did not do that in Execute because I did not consider it important—not because it was a bad idea, but because I did not want to. These games contain many upgrades. Using a skill tree will not make your game less fun. Steal the upgrade structure, mission loops, and currency systems—the parts that are not the focus of the game and have already been solved. This is why I tell people to play more games: they expose you to mission loops. You do not have to reinvent the mission-loop wheel. Armored Core 6 has a mission-loop structure similar to many racing games. I am not saying it copied racing games; it is simply a good mission structure. The developers could have invented something strange and unfamiliar, but that might have made the game harder to understand and less commercially successful. This is what I mean by playing more games: you learn the language of games and recognize which systems, such as mission loops and upgrade structures, have already been solved. We do not need new ways to do these things. As I said, Execute does not have a skill tree. Its skill trees are simply buttons you click. I did that because skill trees overwhelm me. I lose track of what I am upgrading, and I do not find them fun. I wanted to see exactly what I was upgrading for each machine, next to that machine. I am not saying you must steal every established solution. Play many games, then decide what to adopt based on your own experiences and what you have seen other players experience. I am not claiming this was a major innovation. I changed it deliberately after seeing many incremental games use skill trees; I wanted to try something different. Maybe the game would have sold better with a skill tree, but it is a minor feature. The fantasy matters more. Here are more things you could steal from these games when making a game about controlling giant machinery. From farming simulators, we could take clear machine-interaction patterns: tractors pull things across fields. The purpose is clear. You could design a game in which each machine's purpose is clear. There are surely more examples; I have not played much Farming Simulator. Playing these games lets you find them. From Armored Core, you can take the mission structure. This game has one currency, which I love. The more games I play that use one currency to upgrade everything, the more I wonder why I bother with crafting systems. A single-currency system removes friction from upgrades. This system appears in many games, including Armored Core 6. It already works within this machine fantasy, so you can borrow the single-currency economy. This is what you should steal. Do not make a mech game; borrow its currency economy. The upgrade loop between runs involves adding parts to your mech and balancing power and weight. That system could also work in a racing game; it already appears in many racing games. You could adapt it to another machine-based game. There are tactile controls: all the UI is diegetic, meaning the controls and objects you use are realized in the game world. There is also visual complexity that remains intuitive—everything makes sense when you look at it. Other things to steal include the following. Settings menus: do not reinvent the wheel. Unless you are deliberately doing something funny, such as an Undertale-style save system that interferes with reloading saves. Then an original approach is fine. Otherwise, do not put menus where people will not expect them. Common UI conventions, such as tab systems, are fine even if some people dislike them. Borrow UI conventions that people already understand. For example, many players dislike the mouse-controlled interface in Assassin's Creed games. If players dislike that interface on console, do not use it for a console release. Main menus are largely solved, but games such as Signalis show that you can still make them fun and distinctive by subverting a familiar system for comedic effect. Playing many games expands your fantasy lexicon. You encounter new fantasies. Other games may be built around this fantasy. Thinking in terms of fantasy lets you reconsider your favorite games. You may think you want to make a game like Slay the Spire, then realize that you do not like it because it is a card game—you like it because it is about climbing a giant spire. As in Hunter X Hunter, climbing a spire is a compelling concept. You could make an entire game about it. Be careful: Tower of Guns already uses this idea. You climb a tower made of guns and collect guns. Do not mistake that for an original idea: Tower of Guns already uses it. You expand your fantasy lexicon. You also gain mechanical vocabulary, such as single-currency economies. If you cannot discuss games this way or break them into their component parts, making games will be harder. This is not gatekeeping; anyone can make games. But the more vocabulary you gain from playing, discussing, and thinking about games, the more useful these concepts become. That is why I make these videos. People say, "Thank you for making these videos." The videos are for me: they force me to organize my thoughts digitally and discuss them, which solidifies my ideas and makes me a better developer. The main reason to have a development YouTube channel is accountability for your ideas. This vocabulary is important because you can examine the games you have played and the games you are making, then mix and match ideas or analyze games. With developer teammates, you can use it to extract, add, or remove specific mechanics. Playing more games also reveals solved friction points. When you compare old and new games—old and new platformers, for example—you can see which friction points have been solved. In platformers, one example is the life system. In the original Super Mario Bros., dying three times without earning a 1-Up sends you back to the beginning. In newer platformers, the life system is largely gone, and checkpoints are generous because punishing players that much is not fun. Not every platformer follows this pattern, but it is a solved issue you can adopt—if you have played enough platformers to notice it. It increases recombination potential. Playing many games is a good way to create fantasies by combining existing ideas in new ways. That is the process: define your fantasy, find similar games, identify what works, extract frameworks, and rebuild them in your own context. This is the important part: do not clone; rebuild in your own context. When a fantasy works, as with Vampire Survivors, many people make clones. But rebuilding that fantasy in a new context can also succeed. Other Vampire Survivors clones were essentially the same top-down 2D game. This one is similar, and the creator says in his devlogs that he borrowed from Vampire Survivors.
